# The copy standard

Operational companion to `03-copy-research.md`, which holds the evidence for every rule here. Each section pastes into a system prompt on its own.

---

## 1. The standard

Argument

1. Sell one thing to one reader on one promise with one reason to believe it. The promise is the reader's outcome in the reader's terms: the result they buy the product for (the rankings, the customers, the sales, the money, the hours back, the state they want), or the finished thing itself when that is what they buy (a site built, a book written). The product's category and internals (that it is AI, its steps, its reviews, its research) are reasons to believe, placed in the body; they never are the promise, the headline or the deck's opening unless the reader's own verbatims ask for them by name. Crossheads follow the same rule: each states what the reader gets, and the feature is its reason in the body; a run of crossheads naming what the product does is a feature list. In a short piece (organic social, TikTok/Reels, Meta ad, Google RSA, a single, sequence or cold email, SMS) the internals are one sentence of proof at most, said as what the reader gets from them ("it checks who already ranks for each keyword and what it would take to pass them"); the piece never lists or counts the product's steps ("It takes seven steps," "seven stages, in this order:"), and a draft that promises steps cuts the promise. Write the thesis first: "This draft exists to prove that [mechanism] is a different and superior way to get [promise] for [reader], and the proof is [carrier]."
2. Take the desire from the market's verbatims; every description of the reader's state maps to one.
3. The headline and lead are set by the diagnosed awareness state of the traffic segment, never by the format.
4. At sophistication stage 3 or higher the argument is a mechanism, stated in two parts (why the usual fixes fail, why this one works), in one step, with intuitive and empirical proof.
5. Find the big idea by search: the unused fact, the opposite of the category's idea, the mechanism in the process.
6. The lead contains one claim a skeptic would doubt.
7. Lead with the strongest claim the reader accepts without argument, then climb. Never violate an existing belief in the headline or deck.

Proof

8. Sell the result. The piece promises what the reader ends up with (the rankings, the customers, the sales, the money, the hours back) plainly, in the indicative, at full strength ("Close more of the roofing jobs you already quote."). Proof is whatever makes that believable, strongest first: a demonstration the reader runs or watches (the trial on their own numbers, a documented example); a result with its numbers; the mechanism said as the cause ("it texts every quote back within five minutes"); reason-why logic the reader already accepts ("a homeowner with a leak hires whoever calls back first"); process specifics; the exact output the reader gets; the founder's conviction, said as his call ("I wouldn't send a quote without it"); a risk reversal that makes trying cheap. An offer with no customer results on file is sold on everything in that list but the results, at the same strength, and the copy never mentions what is not on file. "Never make the claim bigger than the proof" means one thing: a number, a rate or a time on a result ("twice the jobs," "booked within a week") or a result someone got needs that data in the ledger; without it the sentence promises the result with no number, and never invents one. A quality judgment the owner keeps (better, clearer, stronger than an alternative) is the owner's claim: the speaker states it in the first person as his call, at full strength, next to the way the reader can check it (a free trial, the guarantee).
9. Every claim carries its proof within two sentences. No proof box, no proof wall at the end.
10. Precision matches the evidence: 2,146 when the ledger holds 2,146, "about 2,000" when it holds about 2,000. Precision is about numbers; it never sizes a promise down ("some," "could," "might," "may"), never asks for a detail that weakens the proof when the claim stays true without it (rule 12), and a reviewer never asks for one ("with no website yet").
11. Every study, number, name, credential, quote, price and deadline carries a ledger ID. A ledger row is the fact, not the wording: the copy keeps its numbers, names, and every word that sets a claim's size or precision ("up to," "about," "most," "within"), and says the rest the way the speaker would to one reader, never in the row's feature phrasing ("judged against the pages you would have to beat," "links to the ranking pages it read"); the user's accepted wording, a line the offer block says to repeat (a page's guarantee sentence, a bonus name, the button), and a quotation of a documented person or source stay as written. A verbatim tells the writer what the reader lived and which words matter to them; the copy says that thing as something that happened, using only details a verbatim or the story inventory holds ("pages you paid for that never earned back what they cost"), keeps how often and how much the verbatims say ("some of," "most of"), borrows a verbatim's words only where they are vivid and specific, and never uses its survey wording ("poor results," "for the price") or hedges the reader's own pain. A verbatim with no ledger row is never quoted or attributed ("one buyer wrote," "a reviewer said").
12. At most one damaging admission per piece, and only when the brief holds one: a true, minor flaw that makes the promise easier to believe (a price, a look, a limit this reader does not care about), followed at once by the claim it licenses. Never admit the absence of proof, tests, results, customers or reviews; that is not an admission, it is a confession, and it is a Tier 0 tell. Never volunteer a detail that weakens the proof when the claim stays true without it: "a pilot with three clients, none paying yet" is "a pilot with three clients". When a line is true only with the weakening detail, rewrite the line so it is true without it ("the subscription the plan was built for," not "the product it plans to sell").
13. Testimonials are documented people with a specific result, placed beside the claim they support. Never generate a testimonial, a composite or a narrator.

Offer

14. Design the offer before the brief: variant from the options sheet, every term a ledger row, a denominated guarantee, a deadline with a mechanism.
15. Write the order form first and read it first. The close, the order form and every email state the identical deal.
16. State the price once, after value, without apology, with its reframe and guarantee adjacent. A piece that does not take payment (a short piece, rule 1, or a podcast read) states the offer once, in one line (the price, the trial and the action), where the format puts it (the CTA, the first comment with the link, an ad's headline field), and leaves the full terms, the trial's limits (what opens when), the cancel mechanics and the refund policy to the page that takes the billing details. There the low price of trying is the risk reversal ("a dollar to try it on your own numbers"); a guarantee appears only when the offer leads with it, as one plain promise in the speaker's words ("your plan finishes, or your credits come back"), never its conditions, units or mechanics.
17. Every discount, bonus value, deadline and scarcity figure has a reason-why and a ledger basis.
18. One call-to-action path: what to do, what happens after the click, what arrives when.

Voice

19. One named person talks to one reader; the reader knows who and why by word 150.
20. Write in the indicative. The speaker says what the product does and what he does, never what it could do or what he would do ("I'd start with," "could bring," "would have had you"). A hedge on a promise its proof carries is a defect.
21. Use the market's words.
22. Never ask a question and answer it yourself ("The result? More sales." "Why does this matter? Because..."). Make the point. A question in the copy is one the reader answers about their own situation, and the next line does not answer it for them.
23. Read it aloud; rewrite any sentence you would not say across a table. Plain words: "use," not "utilize"; "try," not "endeavor." No formal connectives ("furthermore," "moreover," "additionally," "in addition"); a new sentence or paragraph does that job. Break a wall of text into short paragraphs; when every sentence runs the same medium length, break some up and let a few run long.
24. Every sentence does a job (claim, proof, mechanism, picture, transition, instruction, identification) or is cut.
25. Story carries a claim or a proof; beats join with "therefore" or "but," never "and then." A story is the client's documented story, a framed hypothetical, or a sourced public anecdote.
26. Close long and literally: what the reader gets by acting now, what they lose by waiting, what to do.
27. Open on the point and end on it. No windup ("In today's world," "Today I want to talk about"), no "In conclusion," no summary paragraph or closer that restates what the piece already said, no restated subject lines, no cheerful sign-offs.
28. The tells and words in Section 7.1 stay out of every piece, as patterns: a synonym or near-variant of a banned pattern is the same tell. No em dashes (a period, a comma or parentheses), no emojis, no hashtags, anywhere. One tell on its own is not the giveaway; the stack is. Get each piece as close to zero as the writing allows without mangling it: rewrite the sentence so it reads well and still loses the tell.

Process

29. Draft in this order: offer block and order form; bullets and proof; body; three leads; headline last.
30. Write three leads on three reader hypotheses, cut each from the top, cold-read them on a different model, pick one, rewrite it.
31. Every draft and every revision passes the tell edit (Section 7.1) before the cold read.
32. Review against a control: beat, lose or unsure, with the one fatal defect. No additive score decides anything.
33. Log the prediction and the result, including refunds at 30 days.

## 2. Argument selection

1. Generate twenty candidates per offer by search (the unused fact, the opposite of the category idea, the mechanism in the process), one line each: core desire [cluster, k] | promise | mechanism.
2. Compute C1-C3 per cluster (`research/rubric/magnitude.py`): urgency = share of verbatims with intensity or time-pressure markers, banded 0-3 at 10/25/50 percent; staying power = the higher of the duration-marker share banded at 10/30/50 and distinct years banded at 2/3/4; scope = prevalence banded at 5/15/30 percent. A share band needs the count behind it: band 1 needs two marked verbatims, band 2 three, band 3 five; a small cluster stays at the band its count supports, so three intense quotes in a cluster of six do not outrank seventeen quieter ones. Years and prevalence are counts already.
3. Gates, before any sum. Desire: C1+C2+C3 ≥ 4 of 9 with k ≥ 5 verbatims (fewer passes only as provisional). Proof: C9 ≥ 2, meaning the ledger holds a verifiable fact that covers the promise.
4. Score survivors on C4-C14. Weighted total = Σ weight × score, maximum 57.
5. Tie-break: C9, then C3, then C14. Still tied, present both.
A promise that restates the mechanism, or names the product's category or one of its steps, is a mechanism, not a promise: C5 and C11 score at most 1. The promise is the reader's result in the purpose's terms (the rankings, the customers, the money), never the product's deliverable or output, whether or not results are on file. C9 counts every carrier in rule 8: a feature, process or mechanism row that produces the result is a verifiable fact, a demonstration the reader runs makes claim and proof the same object, and a promise of the result needs no result row (a number or rate in it does). C8 is judged against what the reader's current tool or method already gives them, features as well as claims: a mechanism built from things the reader already has (a column, a score, a filter their tool shows) scores at most 1.

6. Output three finalists, each with the lead verbatim, the belief to displace, the mechanism mapped to ledger rows and the proof asset. Keep the two losers as body reasons and the next two lead tests.
7. A stated purpose. When the user states what the piece is for, each candidate carries its fit: named (its promise is the desire or big idea the purpose states, in the purpose's terms), serves (it advances the purpose's outcome by another desire) or none. The argument is chosen among the named survivors when there are any, else among those that serve; the rest are kept, ranked below, with the reason. Among named survivors the user has already chosen the desire, so C1-C3 do not rank one over another: they are ranked on C4-C14. A named or serving candidate passes the desire gate as provisional when the research is thin or silent on its cluster, so the user's steering is never failed by evidence alone; the proof gate still applies.

## 3. Lead selection by awareness level

Diagnose the state per traffic segment. If the verbatims never contain the category word, the market is not solution-aware.

| State | Traffic | Lead type | Opening move | Product named | Do not |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Most aware | Returning buyers, branded search, cart | Offer | Product, price, deal, deadline in line one | Line one | Explain the problem |
| Product-aware | Retargeting, trial list, pricing page | Promise | The promise with the mechanism named; a new reason to act | First 100 words | Re-educate; story lead |
| Solution-aware | Category search, demo page, comparers | Promise, or Secret when known solutions are distrusted | Name the desire, show a solution is possible, then the mechanism | After the mechanism | Open with the product name |
| Problem-aware | Cold social, cold email, content traffic | Problem-Solution (direct) or Proclamation | Name the need in the reader's words; dramatize only until the next sentence is earned | After the turn | Lead with the solution; "Are you tired of..." |
| Unaware | Cold lists, skeptics | Story or Proclamation (indirect) | A scene, a dated event or an identification line with the big idea inside | After word 300 | Pitch in the first 300 words |

Direct leads: Offer, Promise, Problem-Solution. Indirect: Story, Proclamation, Secret; an indirect lead must be interesting on its own. Skeptical readers get an indirect lead even when aware. The lead is 300-600 words in long copy, ends on an open loop, names the mechanism at most, and identifies the speaker by word 150. Draft three leads of different type; cut from the top; cold-read; pick one and rewrite it.

Hooks. Where the opening line is the whole lead (an organic social post, a TikTok or Reels script, a YouTube ad, a short VSL), write three hooks the same way: three reader hypotheses, three lead types, cold-read, pick one. The hook says the reader's problem plainly, in words the reader would use: a concrete thing from the reader's world (the tool, the task, the money or the hours) and what it costs them. It states the claim directly: no reframe that presents one thing as another ("Sorting by difficulty feels like research. It's where bad content gets made."), no windup ("Here's the part the score hides"), no teaser that withholds nothing specific ("It starts earlier than you think"). Read it aloud: it is a sentence the speaker would say to one reader across a table, not a line for a poster. A number about the product's own output is the proof in the next line, never the hook. The speaker's credentials and the product's name wait for the body; a first-person line about what the speaker saw or did is fine.

## 4. Proof placement

1. Hunt proof before writing the lead; grade every item A-F (Section 8). Each promise rides on the strongest carrier the hunt finds (rule 8); a number, a rate or a result someone got with no row behind it is not written.
2. Place proof where the doubt arises: a hint in the headline; the first carrier at the first big promise; every later claim followed by its carrier within two sentences; the admission just before the strongest claim; the guarantee after the price.
3. Strength order: demonstration; specific result with denominator; mechanism; reason-why logic the reader accepts; process specifics; named authority with ledger ID; documented testimonial; case history; the founder's conviction; candor; exact social-proof counts. Vague social proof, adjective stacks and generic testimonials are cut.
4. By format: order form carries the guarantee and one buying-experience testimonial beside the button; email carries one claim and one carrier; VSL and webinar put the demonstration in the first third; in an ad the proof is the headline; upsell carries the mechanism plus one specific.
5. Testimonials carry name, place, number, time and one imperfection, sit beside the claim they support, and carry the typical result adjacent when they state a result.
6. B2B: engineers get specifications and precise nouns; finance gets the cost chain; ops and marketing get named customer proof.
7. No customer results on file (a new product, or results never collected): sell the result on the rest, strongest first: the demonstration the reader runs (the trial on their own case, told as exactly what they will see and how soon); the mechanism as cause; reason-why logic; what the reader's current way costs them, from the verbatims; the exact output; process specifics; the maker's own use and logged results, failures included; the founder's conviction; a risk reversal that makes trying cheap. The space a result would fill goes to these, never to the product's internals or to a point said again.
8. The proof proves the promise in the promise's own terms (buyers, money, a decision the reader can check), never with the measure the piece argues against: a post that says traffic numbers do not pick pages does not prove itself with search volume. Describe the proof's subject by what it is and sells ("an espresso bean subscription"); never add that it is new, planned, unlaunched or has no site yet when the claim stays true without it. A demonstration that shows only what the reader would have guessed (a coffee brand told to build coffee pages) proves nothing: use the call the reader's current way would not have made, or let the reader's own run be the proof.

## 5. Offer presentation

Construction, before the brief: choose the variant from the options sheet (hard offer; bill-me or installments; free trial; money-back; charter window; premium-led; continuity; multi-unit) and record why; name the first visible win and its interval; aim a component or the guarantee at the biggest reason the buyer would fail; make the bundle uncomparable; denominate the guarantee; give the deadline a mechanism (bonus expiry, price-rise date, cohort start, capacity cap) or drop it; check what the buyer is told arrives first against the first-week experience (the copy still sells the result the product produces, rule 8).

Sequence: (1) transition that names the thing; (2) the core offer, named with outcome and interval, shown worth the price alone; (3) the value frame; (4) bonuses, each answering a named worry, dollar values only with a ledger basis; (5) the price, once, then the reframe, with per-day frames only when comparable and the full price disclosed, and price-drop rungs that are real reference prices; (6) the guarantee, denominated, as a sentence the buyer can repeat; (7) the reason to act now with its mechanism; (8) the CTA with high sidewalls; (9) objections after the first CTA, each with proof and the restated guarantee; (10) second CTA and P.S.

Must not: feature list as offer; fabricated stack values; apology at the price; a bolt-on "30-day money-back, no questions asked"; dateless urgency; evergreen timers by default; guilt-trip declines; two things sold at once; "investment in yourself," "incredible value."

## 6. Copy chief rubric

Posture: predict whether a skeptical stranger sends money and keeps it. Review against a control or the strongest competitor promotion. Stop at the first fatal defect. Never average.

Before step 1, the draft has passed the tell edit (Section 7.1); the reviewers read the edited copy.

Step 1, cold read, on a different model with no brief, memo or ledger. Read the skim path alone (headline, deck, crossheads, bold, captions, P.S., order form) and report what is sold, to whom, for how much, and why believe it. Then read the full draft once, fast; report where attention dropped, the one claim you disbelieve, and the promise in one sentence.

Step 2, gates in order; stop at the first failure.

Gate A, argument. Write "This sells [product] to [one reader] on the promise [one thing], and the reason to believe it is [mechanism/proof]." Fail if any slot is blank or plural; if the promise slot is the product's deliverable, output or a feature rather than the reader's result; or if the readback does not match the argument memo.

Gate B, belief and lead. Fail if the headline or deck asserts anything the prospect does not yet accept; if the headline can be answered "no" or "so what"; if the lead opens with a pitch, a definition or a generic description of the reader's world; if the speaker is not identified by word 150; if the lead contains nothing a skeptic would doubt; if the product is named earlier than the awareness state allows; if the lead type does not match the awareness field.

Gate C, claims. Fail if any number, name, study, quote, credential, price or deadline lacks a ledger entry at the ledger's precision; if a promise is hedged ("could," "might," "some," "I'd"); if the piece never says what the reader ends up with (the rankings, the customers, the money) and describes the product instead; if a headline promise is never paid off; if a description of the reader's state has no verbatim (the verbatim supports the substance; the copy says it concretely in the speaker's words, never in the verbatim's survey wording); if an objection is invented or a listed one unanswered; if the offer's numbers differ across assets; if the copy tells the buyer something arrives sooner than it does (the first visible win and its interval). Naming the result the product produces is never outrunning the first week: a plan is sold on the rankings that come later, and the copy says what arrives first.

Gate D, voice. Fail on the read-aloud test; assistant register; "we/our" in place of a named "I" to "you"; a "That's why we built" turn with no cause; two or more Section 7.1 tells or words in the piece (the stack); any em dash, emoji or hashtag; a tell score at hard fail (Section 7); in a short piece (Section 1, rule 1), a list or count of the product's steps or the same point made twice with nothing new the second time (Section 7.1 structure, with its exceptions).

Sentence-job audit alongside the gates: tag every sentence by job; list untagged sentences; flag paragraphs with no claim and no proof.

Step 3, verdict and calibration log. Record the seven observations (argument, proof, pacing, offer, specificity, reader focus, rhythm), the FK grade and the tell score; they never decide the verdict.

```
VERDICT: REJECT at Gate N | REVISE | APPROVE for factual review
PREDICTION vs CONTROL: beat | lose | unsure, with one reason
ARGUMENT READBACK: "This sells ___ to ___ on the promise ___ because ___." (matches memo? Y/N)
SKIM-PATH READBACK: what the crossheads, bold and P.S. alone sell, and for how much
WHAT WORKS (2-3 items, quoted, kept on purpose)
THE ONE THING (the single defect most likely to kill response)
   Quote: "<exact line(s)>"
   Fails: <gate or rule, one sentence>
   Do: <direction; at most one example rewritten line>
SECONDARY NOTES (max 5; each quote / fails / do)
DO NOT TOUCH (lines the revise step may not "improve")
TESTABLE DISPUTES (taste, not rule: propose the one-variable A/B)
CALIBRATION LOG: seven observations, FK grade, tell score
```

After the third reviewed revision, one more revision applies the last review's notes (THE ONE THING and the secondary notes); it passes the tell edit and the mechanical and factual checks and ships without another review, and the user is told the last notes were applied and not reviewed again.

Reviewer instructions: start with what works, briefly. Quote, name the failure, give direction; one rewritten line per note; in a short piece a direction never asks for the product's steps to be listed or counted, it asks for what the reader gets from them; a direction never asks for a verbatim's wording in the copy, it asks for the concrete thing the verbatim describes; a direction never asks for a detail that weakens the proof when the claim stays true without it (rule 12), whatever a source page says; a direction never sizes a promise down or adds a hedge ("some," "could"), it strengthens the carrier instead (rule 8); in a short piece a direction never asks for the guarantee, the terms or the trial's limits (rule 16); every example line follows the rules the copy follows (Section 7.1, rule 16); DO NOT TOUCH never holds a line the format leaves to the payment page (the trial's limits, the terms) or a line built to be quoted; no wholesale rewrites; no style notes unless style is the defect. The revise step changes only referenced lines and honors DO NOT TOUCH. Rewrite passes run separately: strategy, style, passion and clarity, fewest words.

## 7. AI tell checklist

Score = sum of weights per 300 words (Tier 0 weight 10, Tier 1 weight 4 per hit, Tier 2 weight 2 per hit above the density allowance, Tier 3 weight 1 above its allowance and only when a Tier 1 or 2 flag exists; texts under 300 words are scored as 300). Review flag at 6; hard fail at 10, on any Tier 0 hit, or on any format hard gate. The ledger match is the primary detector; vocabulary is a tiebreaker. Thresholds were first measured on 107 Claude drafts and then recalibrated on 899 public human direct response documents (`research/tells/human_calibration_report.md`); the values below are the recalibrated ones. Density allowances use at least 500 words, so one hit in an SMS is never a density. The owner's list in 7.1 governs wherever it and the calibration disagree: em dashes, emojis and hashtags never ship, and the 7.1 tells and words are scored even where human copy uses them.

Tier 0, hard reject

| Tell | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Assistant residue | "Notes: swap in your product name." | Delete |
| Bracket placeholder | "[Product Name] helps [target audience]" | Fill from the ledger or stop |
| Unledgered authority, study, number, name or credential | "A Harvard study found 73% of..." | The ledger item, or cut the claim |
| "AI" in copy for a non-AI product | "our AI-powered approach" | Describe the mechanism |
| Proof confession: announcing that a test, result, proof or customer does not exist | "No blind test has been run, so I won't tell you it sells better." | Cut it. Make only the claims the ledger carries; absence of proof is never copy (rules 8 and 12). Zero hits in 871,661 words of human direct response |
| Markdown bold-label-colon bullets (`**Label:**`) or markdown headings in email, video, message, social and audio formats | "**Speed:** get reports in minutes" | A sentence with the claim and its carrier. Plain-text "Label:" lines are human-normal (92% of human sales pages) and only count at Tier 3 above 3 per 1,000 words |
| Em dash (7.1, tell 11), including a spaced en dash or a double hyphen used as one | "It works — fast." | A period, a comma or parentheses |
| Emoji anywhere, check marks included (7.1, tell 12) | "✅ Instant access" | Remove it; never add one |
| Hashtag (7.1, tell 13) | "#SEO #marketing" | Remove it; never add one |

Tier 1, weight 4

| Tell | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| "That's why we built" | "That's why we built Reportly." | A reason-why turn: discovery, failed fixes, cause |
| "You're not alone" / "If that sounds familiar" | "If that sounds familiar, you're not alone." | The concrete thing the reader lived, from the research, in the speaker's words (rule 11) |
| "less than a cup of coffee" | "for less than your daily latte" | A comparable comparison with the full price stated |
| "In the next few minutes I'll show you exactly" / "Stay with me" | VSL opener | The interrupt tied to the promise |
| Summary closer | "In short, X gives you A, B and C." | The CTA and what happens after the click |
| Self-posed question header | "### Why only $49?" | A crosshead that states the answer |
| Any 7.1 tell (1-10), word or structural windup, per hit | "Here's the kicker:" | The 7.1 fix |

Tier 2, weight 2 per hit above the allowance, per 1,000 words, by format profile (email = single email, sequence, cold email, SMS; social = organic social, TikTok/Reels; optin; page = every page, commerce, video and audio format; ads carry the page values): rule of three above 4 (email, social), 7 (page, ads), 11 (opt-in); trailing participles (", giving you...") above 2.0; hedges in body copy ("usually," "often," "may") above 2.0, testimonial and disclosure blocks excluded (hedges inside claim sentences are the factual review's job); "actually / exactly / every single" above 2.0 (4.0 for opt-in and social); anaphora triads above 1.5 (exempt in social); "designed to / built for / crafted / tailored" above 1.0; "cancel anytime / no contracts / no setup fees" beyond its first mention (the offer block states it once); negative parallelism ("It's not X. It's Y.") above 1.0, allowed when it corrects a belief the reader holds; "X. Not Y." clips above 2.0; generic emotion words as the only emotional content ("frustrated," "overwhelmed," "juggling") above 1.0 (replace with the consequence scene); "Imagine..." or a question opener with no specific on the next line, and "Most X don't ... because A. They ... because B.": any hit. "No X. No Y. Just Z.", "not just X but Y" and "Here's the kicker" moved to 7.1.

Tier 3, weight 1, never alone: the 4o-era phrases ("Picture this"), the vocabulary that still separates AI drafts and is not in 7.1 ("streamline," "state-of-the-art," "supercharge," "unparalleled"), plain-text "Label:" lines above 3 per 1,000, and "rather than" above 6.67 per 1,000. Genre vocabulary human copy uses as much as AI does and 7.1 does not list is not scored: "transform," "empower," "effortless," "journey," "holistic," "innovative," "peace of mind," "stress-free."

Argumentative tells, gate failures: no central argument; wrong awareness level; benefits with "so you can"; no mechanism; proof absent or dumped at the end; invented objections; generic specificity ("over 10 years," "award-winning," "up to 50%"); the obvious angle; prevention framing; a lead with nothing to disbelieve; a headline promise never paid off; a turn with no cause.

Tonal tells: "you may want to consider," "it's worth noting"; both-sides framing; no stance; "You deserve this"; apology at the pitch transition. Structural flag: sentence-length coefficient of variation under 0.70 in 300+ words for pages, commerce, video and audio formats, and under 0.50 for email, message and social formats (78% of long human emails sit under 0.70).

Not tells, never penalize: exclamation marks; ellipses; ALL-CAPS words; fragments; "Dear Friend"; "P.S."; superlatives with proof. (Human direct response uses em dashes, "Here's what / how / why" and "not just X but Y"; the owner's list bans them anyway, 7.1.)

Overrides: Meta ads allow caps; opt-in and upsell blocks allow five plain bold lead-ins with no colon; VSL allows "keep watching" once; Google RSA headlines allow no caps and no exclamation marks. Meta ads have no human control yet (no public ad text could be collected), so they carry the page thresholds until one exists.

### 7.1 The owner's tells and words

These are the owner's rules. They apply to every piece in every format: the writer follows them, the tell edit removes what slips through, the detector counts them, and the copy chief fails a stack at Gate D. Each is a pattern, not a word list: rewording a banned pattern with a synonym or near-variant is the same tell.

| # | Tell | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Quietly" for something that was not a secret | "Legiit quietly passed a million orders." | Cut it, or say who did not know |
| 2 | The three-beat that lands on a deep third | "Not a tool. Not a course. It's a system." | State the claim once: "It's a system." |
| 3 | A rhetorical question the copy then answers itself | "The result? More sales." / "Why does this matter? Because..." | Make the point: "You make more sales." |
| 4 | "Here's the kicker / the thing / the part nobody talks about," and any "Here's the ___" windup, reworded ones included: a label ("My rule:", "The fix:", "Bottom line:") or a count of what is coming ("Two questions decide it.", "I'd ask two things."), and in a short piece a count of steps, reasons or ways ("It takes seven steps.", "seven steps, in this order:", "Three reasons to switch:"), though a long format's announced structure (a webinar's three secrets, a listicle's numbered reasons, a VSL's plan) is not a windup; a colon reveal ("it leaves the decision with you: whether your site can beat them") or a "What ... is" opener ("What comes back is a decision:") | "Here's the part the score hides:" / "The score hides this:" / "My rule: build only the pages you can win." | Make the point directly |
| 5 | "Worth noting / remembering / keeping in mind" | "It's worth noting that the trial is free." | State the point: "The trial is free." |
| 6 | The buzzword pile | "double down," "at scale," "best in class," "move fast" | Keep one precise term, cut the rest |
| 7 | "Genuinely" and "meaningfully" as filler, and the same filler in other words ("truly," "really," "actually," "exactly," "literally") | "a genuinely useful report" / "exactly what your list needs" | Cut the word when the sentence means the same without it |
| 8 | The disguise frame: one thing presented as another thing in disguise | "X wearing a Y costume," "cosplaying as Y," "X dressed as Y," "X wrapped in Y," "X masquerading as Y," "X disguised as Y," "X in Y's clothing," "X with a Y veneer, skin or wrapper," "Sorting by difficulty feels like research. It's where bad content gets made." / "The keyword was never your competition. The pages ranking for it are." | State the claim directly: "X is really Y" or "X is just Y" |
| 9 | "The uncomfortable truth / the hard truth / the truth nobody wants to hear" | "The hard truth: most pages never rank." | Drop the signpost; a plain "honestly" once is fine |
| 10 | The pressure test | "stress test this," "sanity check," "pressure test that" | Say what is checked and how |
| 11 | Em dashes | "It works — fast." | A period, a comma or parentheses |
| 12 | Emojis | "✅ Instant access" | Remove them; never add them |
| 13 | Hashtags | "#SEO #marketing" | Remove them; never add them |

Words to cut, or replace with the plainest word that keeps the meaning (cut it when the sentence works without it):

- AI words: delve, tapestry, realm, navigate, navigation, unlock, seamless, elevate, landscape, testament, bustling, harnessing, ever-evolving, ever-changing, multifaceted, meticulous, bespoke, tailored, captivating, enthralling, beacon, unleash, enrich, poised, towards, underpins, demystify, insurmountable, unprecedented, robust.
- Corporate filler: paradigm shift, cutting-edge, game-changing, transformative, groundbreaking, revolutionary, synergy, ecosystem, scalable, disruptive, move the needle, circle back, at the end of the day, when it comes to, the world of, not only (and its near-variant "not just X but Y"), next level, optimized, value-add, leverage.
- Guru-speak: abundance mindset, manifest, high vibration, the universe, alignment, holding space, sitting with it, authentic, authenticity, lean into, your truth, intentional, mindful.
- Empty hedges: just my two cents, for what it is worth, in my humble opinion, IMHO (and "in my view," "in my opinion," "if you ask me"), take this with a grain of salt, your mileage may vary.
- Formal words: utilize (use), endeavor (try); formal connectives: furthermore, moreover, additionally, in addition.
- Legal boilerplate: non-waivable rights, legal rights apply, terms (and conditions) apply, not refundable, non-refundable, void where prohibited, subject to change, where permitted by law, all rights reserved, without notice, at our sole discretion, restrictions apply. Sales copy states the offer in plain words; the terms live on the page that takes payment. A disclosure block and the order form are exempt.

Exempt: words inside a quotation (a verbatim, a testimonial or a quoted source keeps its words), a proper noun, and a word that is the literal name of what the offer sells ("landscape lighting" for a landscaper, "wheel alignment" for a garage).

Structure: open on the point, with no windup ("In today's world," "Today I want to talk about"); end on the point, with no "In conclusion," no closer that restates what was already said, and no teaser, at the end or anywhere else, that withholds nothing specific ("earlier than you think," "that's where the trouble starts," "First, though," "I built a different way to pick them."); say each point once: in a short piece, a run of five words or more said again later is cut, or rewritten so the later sentence adds something the first did not, and so is a sentence that makes the same point again in new words; a CTA, a price, a guarantee, a disclosure, a P.S. that restates the offer for the skimmer, an on-screen caption that shows the spoken line, and any repeat the format asks for (a restated guarantee, a repeated CTA, a code and URL said twice) are not repeats; no lead-in built from one-line paragraphs that each land a maxim, a slogan or a credential (the feed's AI cadence): each line says a concrete thing; a credential is never a sentence of its own ("I'm Legiit's founder."), it rides in the sentence that says why it matters ("I founded Legiit, and I stopped letting volume pick pages."); in a short piece the product's internals get one sentence, said as what the reader gets, however the lines are worded; no common word coined as a name for the product or its output ("the read," "the play") when the plain noun says it ("the report"); no line built to be quoted (a maxim, a slogan couplet such as "A keyword tool settles neither." answered by "Legiit Keywords settles both.", an aphorism such as "A keyword list is raw material.", a reframe written as an aphorism such as "every skip is a page you don't pay a writer for", or a "was never" reveal such as "A list of traffic numbers was never going to tell you which pages would.") when the plain sentence the speaker would say across a table makes the point; no formal connectives; vary sentence length (break up a run of medium sentences, let a few run longer); break walls of text into short paragraphs; plain words ("use," not "utilize"; "try," not "endeavor"); flatten formatting the text does not need (bold, headers or bullets) into plain prose.

The rule that matters most: one of these on its own is not the giveaway (real people write "honestly," and "worth noting"); the stack is, several in one piece. Get each piece as close to zero as the writing allows without mangling it. Natural and human beats sterile and rule-perfect: if removing a tell would make a sentence worse, rewrite the sentence so it reads well and still loses the tell.

The tell edit. Every draft and every revision passes this edit before the cold read; the revise step, passage rewrites and the factual review's rewritten lines follow the same rules. The edit removes the tells and words above and keeps everything else: the meaning and the argument (no opinion or strong claim softened); every fact, name, number, date, quote, link and ledger tag exactly as written (it never adds a detail a sentence lacks, and never changes one it has); the speaker's voice and reading level (the same person on a good day, not another writer and not a corporate memo); the piece's language; the structure (sections, headings, lists and paragraph breaks, unless the formatting itself is the problem); and the length (close to the original word count). Copy that is already clean comes back close to unchanged; the edit never invents a problem to look useful. It reports what it changed in three to six plain lines, with no emojis or hashtags.

Scoring: tells 11-13 are Tier 0; every other 7.1 tell, word or windup is Tier 1 (weight 4) per hit (a repeated run of words counts from the second repeat in a piece, since one is common in human posts), and two or more in a piece are the stack the copy chief fails at Gate D and the mechanical pass sends back to revision.

### 8.1 Claims taxonomy

| The reader's situation and its cost ("quotes you sent that nobody chased") | A verbatim (Gate C) | | |
| What the product does: function, output, process | The product itself | | |
| Specific health benefit ("lowers fasting glucose 12% in 8 weeks") | RCT, clinically meaningful | Cite adjacent; say if ingredient-only | Disease claims for supplements; "cure/heal/eliminate" on Meta |

### 8.2 Permitted phrasing by evidence grade

The action is always "rewrite to the highest permitted form at this grade," never "append a disclaimer."

| Grade | Evidence | Permitted form | Forbidden |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | RCT on the finished product at the marketed dose | "In a 12-week placebo-controlled trial of 84 adults, those taking two capsules of [Product] daily saw fasting glucose fall an average of 11 mg/dL; placebo fell 2." Establishment language allowed | Extrapolating beyond the endpoint, population or duration measured |
| B | RCT on the ingredient at the same dose and form | "Each serving delivers 500 mg of [ingredient], the dose used in a 12-week randomized trial of 60 adults in which fasting glucose fell 11 mg/dL. We did not run our own trial." | "clinically tested ingredient" alone; "clinically proven [Product]"; a different extract or combination; a lower dose |
| C | Observational | "People who eat two servings of fatty fish a week have, in large population studies, about 15% lower rates of fatal heart disease. These studies show association, not cause." | Causal verbs; "proven" |
| D | Mechanistic, in vitro, animal | "In lab studies, [compound] blocked the enzyme that breaks down collagen. Whether that happens in people taking [Product] has not been tested." Or verbatim: "The evidence in support of this claim is inconclusive; additional research is necessary." | Present-tense human-outcome verbs; "scientifically formulated"; clinician or journal imagery as proof |
| E | Anecdote, testimonial, traditional use | "Ancient remedy used for centuries to aid digestion. There is no scientific evidence that it works." (second sentence as prominent as the first). A result testimonial carries the typical result beside it. | "Results not typical" as the only qualifier; a range instead of a central figure; completer-only averages |
| F | No outside evidence of results | Any product but a health product: the result it produces, promised at full strength and carried by its mechanism, a demonstration, reason-why logic, process specifics, the founder's call and the guarantee. A health product: ingredients and amounts, sourcing, manufacturing standard, taste, guarantee, price, delivery, who it is for | A number, rate or time on a result, or a result attributed to anyone; for a health product, any efficacy claim |

Earnings form: "Jen made $4,300 in her third month. Of the 1,912 people who joined between Jan 2024 and Jun 2025, 6% reached $4,000 in any month; the median member made $210 in their first 90 days, working about 5 hours a week. Ad spend is not included."

Decorative hedges, never a qualification: "may," "might," "could," "helps," "supports," "promotes," "promising," "preliminary," "Results not typical," "Individual results may vary." Hedges that count: "the evidence is inconclusive," "additional research is necessary," "there is no scientific evidence that it works," "supportive but not conclusive research shows," a stated n, duration, dose, population and average effect. A counted hedge sits in the same sentence or immediately adjacent at the same prominence, and nothing in the same section re-asserts the full claim.

### 8.3 Factual-review procedure

Output per flag: `line → rule (cited) → rewritten line`; a flag with no rewrite is rejected. The rewritten line fixes only what its rule names (a number to its ledger precision, a misquoted name or term, a missing disclosure, an invented item cut) and keeps the promise at full strength: it never sizes an outcome promise down, adds a hedge ("some," "could"), adds a weakening detail ("planned," "new," "no website yet"; when a line overstates its subject, the rewrite drops the overstated words), or announces that a test, result, proof or customer is missing (rules 8 and 12; the proof confession is a Tier 0 tell); and it follows Section 7.1 (no em dash, emoji or hashtag, none of its tells or words). Tests in order: (1) ledger match for every number, study, credential, date, quote, name, price, deadline and quantity, with a visible `[[NEEDS SOURCE]]` where the ledger is silent; (2) strength match for a number, rate or time on a result, a result attributed to anyone, and any health claim, against the ledger grade, rewritten to the form in 8.2 (a promise of the result with no number on it, reason-why logic, the reader's own situation and what the product does are not strength flags); (3) disease screen for supplements (disease name, lay symptom, drug substitution, disease-study citation, imagery); (4) testimonial pack (real person on file, bona fide user, typical result adjacent, connection disclosed); (5) earnings screen for a stated income or earnings figure, or any earnings claim in a business-opportunity offer (documentation, dates, percentage, time and effort, expenses, typical result, adjacent); a promise that the product makes the reader money, with no figure, is the outcome promise; (6) track-record screen (4.41 statement, net with gross, no projection); (7) net impression on headline, lead and offer only, fixing the headline and never the footer; (8) cross-asset consistency with the offer block as source of truth (names, deliverables, price and plan, added costs, the guarantee sentence on a page that takes payment and, when a piece that does not states a guarantee, its condition and remedy (rule 16), deadline, delivery promise, button verb); (9) channel pass (CAN-SPAM, TCPA and CTIA, Meta, Google, native labeling, California B&P 17602); (10) fabrication guard: any name, quote, statistic, study, credential, review or narrator not in the ledger is a hard fail. The reviewer may not add "may," "results vary," generic disclaimers or legal boilerplate ("non-waivable rights apply," "terms apply," "not refundable"), delete specifics the ledger supports, or raise "could be seen as" concerns without a rule; it may add an evidence-state sentence from 8.2. Every flag is a decision for the user, never an action by the reviewer: the output shows the line, the rule and the suggested rewrite, and the user keeps the line, applies the rewrite or drops it. The single exception is the fabrication guard, which catches the engine inventing facts, not the user making claims: the revision cuts fabrications and fixes a line that misquotes the ledger or the offer block (tests 1 and 8); every other flag waits for the user and never enters the copy on its own.

### 8.4 Ledger row and per-offer config

Ledger row: claim text; type (8.1); role in the argument; evidence grade with endpoint, population, dose, duration and effect size; source; precision; typical result; dates, cohort and percentage for earnings claims; material-connection flags; guarantee terms and approver; former prices and component values with basis; scarcity numbers with an enforced flag; deadlines with mechanism; trial and continuity terms; story-claim rows; establishment-signal flag. Per-offer config: vertical; business-opportunity and Notice-recipient booleans; humor policy per format; greeting and edge tolerance; refund window and first-week experience.

## 9. Per-format specs

Sections in order, length band, musts and must-nots. Bands are starting values; the control overrides them.

Long-form sales letter. Headline and deck; lead; credibility lines; problem mechanism; solution mechanism and turn; build-up and reveal; proof block; offer and stack; price; guarantee; urgency; close with CTA three times; P.S. 3,000-8,000 words. Musts: benefits as claim, reason, proof, picture; the skim path sells alone; close at 15-25% of length. Must-nots: bio before the reader cares; proof wall at the end; mechanism explained in the lead; summary close.

Short landing page, product-aware. Headline matched to the ad; subhead with who and differentiator; CTA with the action in the button; three to five argued benefits with proof in line; proof strip; two objections with guarantee; repeated CTA. 150-600 words. Must-nots: problem education; story lead. B2B demo variant adds what the call covers, said as the things the reader gets (not a "here's what you'll get" windup), and an indicated price; 300-750 words.

Opt-in page. Headline with number, timeframe or mechanism; three to six incomplete bullets; email-only form; button completing "I want..."; one proof line. 60-250 words. Must-nots: headline describing the artifact; "Submit"; "we will never spam you."

Upsell / OTO. One-line confirmation of the first decision; bridge to the next problem the purchase creates; the offer as the shortest path, mechanism in a sentence; the true reason it is only here; stack and price; one-line guarantee; Yes button stating the benefit; plain No link. 300-900 words or 3-5 minute video; 1x-3x the first purchase. Must-nots: re-selling the first product; "disappears forever"; resetting timers.

Order bump. Checkbox; bolded "Yes! Add [thing] for only $X"; two or three sentences: what it is, how it makes the main purchase work faster, why only here if true. 30-80 words; 20-40% of the main price. Must-nots: a same-priced bump; an unrelated product.

Order form and checkout. Acceptance statement; recap in the close's words; price, total and every added cost; what arrives and when; guarantee verbatim beside the button; deadline as a fact; trust block in the payment area; one buying-experience testimonial; options highest price first. Under 120 words outside the recap; eight fields or fewer. Must-nots: any bonus, price, tier or claim the close did not state; "Submit"; visible coupon field.

Thank-you page. Confirmation with where the thing is; one instruction for the next sixty seconds; bridge to one low-ticket offer tied to the magnet's promise. 80-250 words.

Single email. Subject written last from the body; hook line; one story or argument; one-sentence bridge; one link; sign-off; P.S. past 250 words. 30-80 words cart-close; 200-350 objection; 350-700 story. Musts: one reader, one idea, one link; greeting per voice profile; subject under 70 characters. Must-nots: subject restated as line one; three benefits with "so you can"; multiple links; "Not this. That."; "Let that sink in."

Sequences. Welcome 5-7: deliver and reframe; origin story; belief shift; proof story; objection email; direct offer. Launch 10-12: three prelaunch; open-cart; four mid-cart (biggest objection, customer story, FAQ, who this is not for); three close-day; post-close. Abandoned cart 3: 30-60 minutes, 24 hours, 48-72 hours, no discount first. Post-purchase 5: fast win; how to get the result; check-in; review request; next offer. Win-back 3: nine-word email with no links; what you missed; stay-or-go. Musts: every non-final email ends with a reason to open the next; every email sells. Must-not: an extended deadline.

Webinar package. Registration: the One Thing, three secrets. Reminders: morning-of, 60 minutes, 10 minutes. Presentation, about 90 minutes: two-agendas statement; five testimonials in five minutes; ruler goals; Epiphany Bridge; case study; three secrets, each with bridge, results, broken beliefs, restated belief; transition with the permission ask; stack; price within five minutes of the offer; guarantee matched to activation points; restack; urgency bonus; 30-minute countdown with live Q&A and closes between questions. Replay: five to seven emails over four days clustered at the deadline. Musts: offer, price, bonuses second-best to best, scarcity throughout, guarantee, objections cycled; 20-26 asks. Must-nots: teaching to completeness; apology transition; invented scarcity; an extended deadline.

Long VSL, warm or retargeted. Interrupt; promise with mechanism; credibility and bribe; dated demonstration; catalyst; short discovery story; the plan with logic per step; objections as personas; transition and stack with the button dropping at the price; price, guarantee, urgency, CTA repeated. No minute allocations; test the first 500 words. Must-nots: biography first; "our proven system"; price before value.

Short VSL, cold. Interrupt tied to the promise; promise, mechanism name and credibility in one breath; catalyst; three fascination-style benefits or a mini-demonstration; who it is for plus one disqualifier; next step; one real reason to act now. 3-7 minutes. Must-nots: stack; price unless the page sells.

Meta and Instagram ads. Primary text 50-150 characters before the fold, headline 27 characters (Instagram 125 and 40). Hook; one angle; one proof; one literal CTA; headline field restating the offer. Musts: 3-6 variations per ad set; describe the product or outcome, never the reader's attribute. Must-nots: "Are you tired of"; "Do you have [condition]"; "cure, heal, eliminate"; effortless-income claims; emojis; hashtags.

YouTube ad script. 0-5s call-out and disqualifier; 5-15s stakes and one credibility line; 15-45s mechanism; 45s+ proof, offer, spoken and on-screen CTA, rehook, second CTA. Under 3 minutes. Must-nots: logo intro; "At [Brand], we"; improbable-result claims.

TikTok and Reels. 15-45 seconds, under 90 spoken words. Line one spoken and captioned (result-first, contrarian or POV); lines two to four show the discovery; one proof; CTA with location. Musts: proposition in the first 3 seconds; a real person on camera. Must-nots: "Hey everyone!"; feature tour; emojis or hashtags in the caption.

Google responsive search ads. 15 headlines of 30 characters: 3-4 keyword, 3-4 benefit with number, 2-3 proof, 2 offer or risk reversal, 2 CTA; 4 descriptions of 90 characters, each a complete argument with CTA. Must-nots: one headline shape; repetition.

Advertorial and native landing page. FTC label above the headline ("Ad," "Advertisement," "Paid Advertisement"); editorial headline; byline and date; scene or surprising fact with no pitch for 150-300 words; the usual fixes widened; mechanism; proof; the turn in the same voice; offer and links repeated in the last third. 800-1,800 words cold; 400-700 retargeting. Listicle variant: number matching the items, reasons escalating problem, mechanism, proof, offer. Musts: label on both pages; narrator exists. Must-nots: brand before word 250; "Promoted" or no label; "stuns," "reveals," "you won't believe"; synthetic before/after.

Organic social. Five lines to earn "see more"; lead-in, main points, CTA; one idea; link in a comment; hook then rehook; three hooks, one picked (Section 3). Musts: a sales intent inside the story or opinion; CTA as verb, object, location. Must-nots: three ideas; "Thoughts?"; emojis; hashtags; a list or count of the product's steps; the lead-in's point said again in the main points (the lead-in states it; the main points carry it forward with new facts). The first comment carries the link and the one-line offer: that is the post's one statement of the offer.

Product description. Name plus benefit tag; a lead in the buyer's words; three to five bullets translating features to situations; proof line; specs in a separate block. 60-150 words for consumables; 150-400 for considered purchases. Musts: phrases checked against the review corpus. Must-nots: "Elevate your [category] experience"; "crafted with care"; superlatives without the proof line.

SMS. [Brand]: [one-idea hook in second person with a stock, scarcity or newness fact] [offer with % and deadline] [link]. 75-115 characters; never above 240. Musts: STOP wording; 8 a.m. to 9 p.m. recipient time. Must-nots: jokes; dollar-off; no deadline; invented "almost gone."

Cold email. Line one, a verifiable observation about the reader; line two, the implied problem or a one-line case study with a number; line three, one question answerable in a word. 40-90 words; subject 1-3 words; no links in email one; follow-ups of one to three sentences, each with one new piece of relevance. Musts: persona-matched proof; tentative over assumptive; 0-1 questions. Must-nots: "I hope this email finds you well"; "I'd love to schedule a call"; "Thoughts?"

B2B pricing page. Value headline; plans as "Best for [persona]"; an offer that neutralizes a researched objection; proof at the decision point; FAQ headings phrased as the objection; three plans; anchor against the expensive alternative; value argument restated above the price. Must-nots: gated pricing without a stated reason; four or more plans; "Contact us" as the only CTA.

Podcast and audio read. Produced spot: 55-70 words; brand within five seconds; one CTA with the code. Host read: talking points, not a script; a "why I use it" moment; one mechanism; one proof; one objection; code and URL said twice, last.

Open, no spec yet: downsell, application and call-booking page, quiz funnel, webinar invitation emails, cancellation flow, UGC video script, X thread, replay page.

## 10. Voice capture

From three to ten samples of the client's own writing, selling copy preferred:

1. Measure sentence-length mean and deviation; share of sentences under six and over thirty words; fragment rate; Flesch-Kincaid grade; paragraph length; first-word class of each paragraph.
2. Extract greeting and sign-off; an idiom bank of 20-40 phrases; a dead-word list; humor type and rate; edge tolerance; formatting habits; pronoun ratio; stance markers; how they name the enemy.
3. Record three verbatim exemplar paragraphs: a lead, a proof passage, a close.
4. Build the story inventory: documented events, dates, numbers, named people and failures, each with a ledger row. This is the part of voice that cannot be imitated.
5. Set the readability band from the audience (consumer grade 5-8; B2B marketing, ops and executive 6-8; finance and legal up to 9-12; engineering plain syntax with precise nouns), then let the sample override it.
6. Write the profile as parameters, never adjectives.
7. Validate: generate 500 words in the profile, re-measure against step 1, and have the client mark every line they would not have written. Adjust the profile, never the draft.
8. Never penalize caps, fragments, exclamation marks or ellipses; human direct response uses all four at rates far above AI drafts.
9. A voice profile never brings back what Section 7.1 bans: em dashes, emojis, hashtags and the 7.1 tells and words stay out even when the samples use them.
