# What makes direct response copy convert (and why AI copy does not)

Research document for the founder and the engineering team; the operational rules distilled from it are in `04-copy-standard.md`. This revision applies two critiques (a completeness review and a copy chief's review) and ten gap-fill research passes to the first synthesis. Claims the fact-checkers rejected are dropped or corrected; the corrections are listed in Section 14.

**How to read the evidence labels.** *Verified* means the wording was fetched from the author's own text or the primary page. *Secondary* means the wording is consistent across reader notes or summaries but the book was not fetched (Caples, Ogilvy's books, Collier, Great Leads, Hormozi, Kobs, Benson's full rule list). *Reported* means a practitioner says a test happened but published no data. *Tested* means a documented split test or dataset with numbers. *Measured* means this project's own corpus analysis (107 AI drafts against 341 human DR documents). *This document* marks a working rule with no source behind it, to be calibrated against the teaching library and the acquired controls.

## 1. Executive summary: the principles that decide whether copy sells

**1. The market and the offer decide more than the words, and the offer is designed, not inherited.** Halbert (verified): "Strong copy will not overcome a weak offer," and "Writing 'copy' is less than 1/10 as important as learning to think about new offers." Kurtz (verified): 41% list or media, 39% offer, 20% creative, "initially." Generic AI takes the product sheet and the deal as given and describes them attractively. A DR shop holds the offer meeting before the brief.

**2. Desire is channeled, not created, and only one desire leads.** Schwartz (verified): "Copy cannot create desire for a product. It can only take the hopes, dreams, fears and desires that already exist... and focus those already-existing desires onto a particular product"; "only one can predominate; only one can reach out through your headline." AI gives every benefit equal weight because coverage feels safe.

**3. The prospect's awareness state fixes where the headline starts.** Schwartz (verified): "a headline which will work to a market in one stage of awareness will not work to a market in another stage." AI writes solution-aware problem-agitate-solve for every traffic source.

**4. Market sophistication fixes whether you need a mechanism.** Schwartz (verified): once the market has heard every claim, "the emphasis shifts from what the product does to HOW it works." AI answers a crowded market by making the same promise louder.

**5. Never make the claim bigger than the proof, and join them.** Bencivenga, Bullet 3 (verified): "Never make your claim bigger than your proof. And always join your claim and your proof at the hip in your headlines." AI hedges claims the evidence supports and inflates claims it has no evidence for. The proof is hunted before the lead is written; a claim with no carrier is sized down, never drafted around.

**6. Specificity is the cheapest proof there is, and precision must match the evidence.** Hopkins (verified): "Platitudes and generalities roll off the human understanding like water from a duck." Caples (verified): "97,482 people have bought one of these appliances" beats "Nearly 100,000"; rounding "52.7%" to "over 50%" killed a campaign. AI writes "thousands" when the ledger holds 2,146, and invents "2,146" when the ledger holds "about 2,000."

**7. The headline and lead win reading, and the lead is the unit the shop tests.** Schwartz (verified): "Your headline has only one job—to stop your prospect and compel him to read the second sentence." Ford (verified): "90% of your success depends on 10% of the copy you write," the "300-500 words that make up the 'lead.'" A pro drafts the lead last, writes several, reads them cold, and throws away the first two pages (Carlton, verified). AI writes the lead first, keeps it, and explains the mechanism inside it.

**8. Every claim, offer and deadline needs a reason-why.** Bencivenga, Bullet 9 (verified): reasons "why your product is superior to other solutions... including doing nothing," reasons "to believe that what you say is true," and reasons "to seize the opportunity today." Halbert (verified): "if you don't have an explanation, your 'deal' won't be believable." AI offers discounts and deadlines with no cause.

**9. Proof goes where the doubt arises, and the lead must contain something to disbelieve.** Schwartz (verified): "Proof—like claims—is most effective when the reader unconsciously demands it," and he rejects "a separate box labelled 'Here's Proof.'" If no sentence in the lead would make a skeptic say "yeah, sure," the copy is too agreeable to sell. AI never earns the proof moment and dumps three generic testimonials after the CTA.

**10. The offer is a proposition the reader cannot compare, with a denominated guarantee and true urgency, and it is identical at the close, the order form and in every email.** Abraham (verified): "Denominate what you're guaranteeing... Speak in terms of specific performance measures." Christensen via Forde (verified): "I write the offer card before anything else." AI writes a feature inventory, bolts on "30-day money-back guarantee, no questions asked," and never writes the order form.

**11. One person talks to one person, as a seller, and the reader knows who by word 150.** Hopkins (verified): "fine writing is a distinct disadvantage... They reveal the hook." Deutsch to a writer (verified): "You put words together nicely – they sound like copy is expected to sound." AI advises ("consider," "it's important to note") because it has no thesis to prosecute and no speaker with a stake.

**12. Story exists to carry a claim; emotion is found in the market, then shown by consequence.** Halbert (verified): "the most-often missing ingredient in a sales message? It's the sales message doesn't tell an interesting story." Makepeace (verified): a faux benefit is "a product feature masquerading as a benefit." AI names emotions ("frustrated," "overwhelmed") instead of inducing them.

**13. The close is long, literal and specific.** Halbert (verified): "Sometimes I devote as 25% or more of the entire ad to closing"; "Tell him what he will get if he hurries and tell him what he will lose if he delays." AI ends with "In conclusion, [Product] is the perfect solution. Click below."

**14. Compliance is net impression plus substantiation; hedges are decoration and evidence-state sentences are the real qualifier.** The FTC (verified): deception is judged on "the entire mosaic, rather than each tile separately"; a disclosure "cannot cure a false claim"; "it's not enough to say that the product 'may' have the claimed benefit or 'helps' achieve the claimed benefit." What the D.C. Circuit accepted in POM Wonderful v. FTC (verified) is an unambiguous statement of the evidence state: "the evidence in support of this claim is inconclusive." AI stacks "may help support" and "individual results may vary" on every claim.

**15. Response net of refunds is the verdict; the reviewer's prediction, scored against results, is the only thing that improves.** Hopkins (verified): "One quickly loses his conceit by learning how often his judgment errs - often nine times in ten." Kurtz (verified): "LTV, not the first sale, is the most important metric." A copy chief predicts beat, lose or unsure against the control, finds the one fatal defect, and logs the prediction.

## 2. The decision hierarchy

Failure higher in this hierarchy cannot be repaired lower down. The worked example is a constructed offer, "Reportly," a client-reporting tool for agencies; it stands in until the control corpus is assembled (Section 13). Real cases are used wherever one exists.

### 2.1 Market and list

Halbert (verified): "The only advantage I want... is A STARVING CROWD!"; "the way to deduce what people want to buy is to simply observe what they DO buy"; "recency is, by far, the most important" list criterion. Benson (verified snippet): "A two-time buyer is twice as likely to buy as a one-time buyer." Perry Marshall's disqualifiers (via Goodreads; the "buy-in to the USP" item was not echoed on the fetched page): money, a "bleeding neck," ability to say yes, fit with their plans. The inputs are who has already paid for something like this, how recently, through which channel, and which competitor controls keep repeating. Kennedy's matchmaking client (via summary) found half the buyers were twice-divorced long-haul truckers; the message had been written for someone else.

Reportly: agencies already pay $300-900 a month for three competing tools, and 1-star reviews of all three cluster around one event, the Monday client call where a stale number gets exposed. A crowd, spending, with a specific wound.

### 2.2 Offer construction, before the brief

This level was missing from the first synthesis. Stone's payment-terms index (tested, reproduced in every edition): cash with order 100, plus free gift 144, bill-me 177, bill-me plus gift 233. Benson (verified snippets): a bill-me offer "will improve results by 50% or more"; "Dollar for dollar, premiums are better incentives than cash discounts"; "Two premiums are frequently better than one"; "You can never sell two things at once." Kurtz (verified) extends the premium rule to digital ("50 are better than four; 100 are better than 50") and notes "our best offers always emphasized the premiums and bonuses above the main product." The counterweights: Kurtz's survey package (record front end, low pay-up; the bookalog that replaced it halved trials and nearly doubled pay-up); Benson: "pay-up is basically a perception of the value of the product"; Schwalm (verified): too many bonuses "can lower response," and "never just change anything in your current control offer without doing a head-to-head test"; Mequoda (vendor): a discounted introductory hard offer now beats soft offers "more than 90% of the time."

Order of tests (Stone, Holtz, Roff-Marsh): offer, then list or traffic, then price, then lead, then format, then body. Inputs: margin, refund history, the buyer's first-week experience, substitute costs, the back end, and the allowable acquisition cost (Benson's "bogey"). Output: the offer options sheet (Section 7.2), the chosen variant, and reasons for price, premiums, guarantee and deadline, each a ledger entry.

Reportly: the 14-day trial converts at 9%; the week-one ticket is "how do I connect the ad account." The offer meeting adds a connection call as the premium, "first report built before you pay," and a 60-day guarantee denominated on connected accounts refreshing on open.

### 2.3 Awareness

Schwartz's five states as he labels them (verified; the popular labels are later shorthand, the five-stage structure is his): (1) "The Most Aware," who "knows of your product—knows what it does—knows he wants it"; headline is product name and price. (2) "The Customer Knows of the Product But Doesn't Yet Want It," "the great bulk of all advertising"; the headline does one of seven jobs, including announcing "a new mechanism in that product." (3) "How to Introduce New Products": "Name the desire and/or its solution in your headline. Prove that that solution can be accomplished. And show that the mechanism... is contained in your product." (4) "How to Introduce Products That Solve Needs": "Start by naming the need... Then dramatize the need so vividly that the prospect realizes just how badly he needs the solution" ("Corns?"). (5) "How to Open Up a Completely Unaware Market": the prospect "is either not aware of his desire or his need—or he won't honestly admit it to himself," so the headline leads with identification or story.

Awareness belongs to a traffic segment. Branded search is state 1-2; retargeting state 2; cold social state 4-5. If the verbatims never contain the category word, the market is not solution-aware. B2B keeps the five states: a demo page is solution-aware, cold email is problem-aware or unaware, a pricing page is product-aware.

Georgi's case (verified) is the wrong decision: ads opened "if you're still looking into things like affiliate marketing, selling stuff on Amazon or dancing on TikTok" to women whose listed pain point was "I'm sick of feeling anxious about my car payment." Reportly's cold Meta traffic is state 4; the trial list is state 2; two leads are required.

### 2.4 Sophistication

Schwartz's five stages (verified): first, "Be simple. Be direct. Above all, don't be fancy" ("NOW! LOSE UGLY FAT!"); second, "copy that successful claim—but enlarge on it. Drive it to the absolute limit" ("LOSE UP TO 47 POUNDS IN 4 WEEKS—OR RECEIVE $40 BACK!"); third, introduce a mechanism ("FLOATS WONDER DRUG FOR REDUCING"); fourth, "elaborate or enlarge upon the successful mechanism" ("FIRST NO-DIET WONDER DRUG FOR REDUCING"); fifth, "identification with the prospect himself" ("WHY MEN CRACK..."). The "discounted 50%" line describes the collapse at the end of stage two and is rhetorical.

Diagnose it from the competitor table: how many make the same promise, whether the controls name a mechanism, whether reviews show exhaustion ("tried three of these"). For SaaS, Wynter's method (verified): one row per competitor and you (category claimed, audience, headline outcome, top three claims with proof); the logo-swap test (a competitor's logo on your hero still reads true means category copy); sort claims into qualifiers and determiners; "Do not invent difference by adding adjectives." Expense management scored 71.6 on sameness (stage 5); applicant tracking 27.8 (stage 2-3).

Brown (verified transcript): "people don't want better, they want different." Reportly's three competitors all promise "beautiful client reports in minutes"; nobody names why reports go stale. Stage 3: the argument must be a mechanism.

### 2.5 Desire

List every performance the product delivers, map each to a mass desire, rank by Schwartz's dimensions, pick one. The gap-fill research computes the dimensions from tagged verbatims (Section 3.5): urgency is the share of a desire cluster's verbatims with intensity or time-pressure markers; staying power is the share with duration markers, or the number of distinct years the cluster appears; scope is prevalence, with a bonus for two independent source types; a cluster needs five verbatims to be eligible. Makepeace's check (verified): "Does your headline trigger a strong, actionable emotion the reader already has about the subject at hand?" Whitman's Life-Force 8 (via summaries) is the checklist of biological wants; convenience and efficiency score low.

The wrong decision is the most frequent generic desire over the most intense specific one. Beachway (self-reported): "If you think you need rehab, you do." beat "Your Addiction Ends Here" by more than 400% on clicks and 20% on form submits. The rubric run found the same shape on three real offers: the two highest-magnitude desires (energy for AG1, returns for Motley Fool) produced the biggest promises and both died at the proof gate. Reportly: "save time on reports" is frequent and mild; "look competent in front of the client" is intense and in the prospect's words, and wins if the product can prove it.

### 2.6 Mechanism

Georgi (verified): "the unique mechanism is always a two-part logical connection," the problem's and the solution's; "They've been missing 1% of the answer, and that's made all of the difference." Criteria (verified): "It's easily digestible and transferable," "It's wildly interesting," "It has two layers of proof" (intuitive, empirical); "Multi-step mechanisms tend to bomb." Schwalm (verified) has "never routinely included" a problem mechanism "like it's some kind of 'rule'," but "finding the unique mechanism of the solution (UMS) usually is needed." Hopkins' Schlitz (verified): process facts common to all brewers worked because "others have never told this story."

The mechanism is found by search, not sampling. Carlton (verified): "A great hook will HIDE from you," and he found one by pushing a client until the client let slip that the product "shows up and embarrasses other people in our market." The copy chief's correction stands: picking the most plausible of twenty generated candidates regresses to the median; the idea comes from the fact nobody has used.

Reportly: reports are built from exports, and an export is stale the moment it runs, so the client sees Thursday's number on Monday; Reportly renders from the live API on open. Intuitive proof: everyone has refreshed a dashboard. Empirical proof: the measured median lag of the three competitors' exports, which the research step must obtain.

### 2.7 Big idea

Ford (verified): "A big idea is an idea that is instantly comprehended as important, exciting, and beneficial," "important to the customer—not the copywriter," with an inevitable conclusion whose "internal logic... is fundamentally simple," and with "durational power." His non-examples: a US-China dollar battle is a big concept with no path to a product; "national decline" alarm makes a reader less likely to buy premium beef. Forde (verified): "Preferably in 8 words or less," and "When you can answer that question so well, that the statement itself could make the sale, you know you're finally ready to sit down and write." Ford's Rule of One (verified, 2025): one reader, one dominant idea, one overwhelming promise, one argument at a time, and it "does not mean you're limited to only one claim or one piece of evidence"; his 2017 AWAI statement (verified) lists five, adding one core emotion, one story and one inevitable response. Deutsch (verified): "A lot of my favorite big ideas are just opposite ideas."

The wrong decision is an idea that needs "and." Lampropoulos's tax promotion (secondhand): "Pay No Taxes in 1997" lost to "Bombshell from top tax attorney: The IRS owes you $10,731. Here's how to get it in as little as 60 days."

The first synthesis gave Reportly "Never walk into a client call with a stale number again," and the copy chief rejected it rightly: the never-X-again template with the mechanism implied and no proof, exactly what AI produces. The argument that survives names the mechanism and the carrier: "Reportly pulls the number the second your client opens the report; the tool you pay for now ships Thursday's export." One desire, the mechanism stated, provable from the lag measurement, no "and."

### 2.8 Lead

Ford's own 2025 essay (verified) settles the direct/indirect split the fact-checker disputed: three direct leads (Offer, Promise, Problem-Solution) and three indirect (Story, "an intriguing statement or prediction," Secret). The first synthesis placed Problem-Solution among the indirect leads; that was wrong. Forde (verified): direct Promise leads work best for "mostly aware" prospects who "know they've got a problem they'd like to solve. And who know, at least vaguely, a product exists that could solve it"; Offer versus Promise is "A HOLLYWOOD SMILE IN 3 DAYS / …OR YOUR MONEY BACK" against the first line alone. Ford: "an indirect lead will only work if it is intrinsically interesting and emotionally appealing." Per-type rules from reader notes remain secondary (a Secret "is not disclosed during the lead"; Problem-Solution stays on the problem only "for exactly as long as it takes to win that commitment to read").

Mapping: Offer for state 1; Promise for states 2-3; Problem-Solution for state 4; Secret for 3-4 when the prospect distrusts known solutions; Proclamation for 4-5; Story for 5. Skeptical readers get indirect leads even when aware. The lead is a 300-500-word object (Ford) and the test unit. Drafting order from Carlton, Schwalm and Deutsch (verified): bullets and proof first, offer and close, body, then three leads of different type on different reader hypotheses, each cut from the top, read cold, one picked and rewritten from scratch (Section 4.4). Reportly cold traffic gets a Problem-Solution lead and a Proclamation lead on the stale-export fact, read against each other; the trial list gets a Promise lead with the mechanism named.

### 2.9 Structure

Copy is as long as the sale requires. Hopkins (verified): "whether long or short, an advertising story should be reasonably complete," and "brief ads are never keyed." CRE's Crazy Egg (+30%) and Moz (+52%) are long wins; NextAfter's long Facebook copy converted 0.50% against 0.35% (n=891,721, an ebook offer); Unbounce 2024 (correlational) finds word count correlating -18.6% with conversion and 5th-7th grade pages converting at 11.1% against 5.3%. Copyhackers (verified): "as long as is necessary to make the argument." MECLABS (verified, no numbers): long wins with "a rational, analytical, need-oriented motivation"; short wins with "low perceived risk, low cost, and low commitment."

### 2.10 Proof

Inventory, grade and place every proof asset before drafting (Section 6), and obtain the assets first. Schwalm (verified) reads "the full-text because that's where you'll find the detail and numbers" and asks "do I have back-up to support this that would stand up in court if need be?"; Rogers (verified) recorded a Loom tour after a prospect wrote "Your sales letter is great. But we can't see what's inside." The proof hunt (Section 3.6) runs against the three largest claims of each candidate; a claim with no carrier is demoted.

### 2.11 Offer presentation

Abraham's flashpoint audit (verified): "identify in the transaction every tangible and intangible... flashpoint. Risk point. Point of concern. Point of impediment," and the real competitor is "doing nothing." Section 7 gives the sequence and checklist.

### 2.12 Close and order form

Halbert (verified): the close may run a quarter of the piece, leads the reader "by the hand," and ends "RIGHT NOW! TODAY!" Kennedy's Step 14 (verified title) makes the P.S. "a second headline." The order form is written first (Christensen via Forde, verified) so the letter has "something to aim for," and read first by the chief because it states what is actually sold; Baymard (tested): 70.22% average cart abandonment, and among non-browsing abandoners 40% cite added costs and 18% forced account creation. Section 5.6 gives the spec and the cross-asset consistency check.

## 3. Research protocol

### 3.1 The governing idea

Halbert (verified): "read and reread every ad or direct mail package that has already been written about what you are trying to sell"; "If it's a book, read it. If it's a product, examine it. If it's a service, use it and ask questions"; one fact per card, product cards reviewed first, then the idea cards shuffled until "a central selling idea" emerges. Copyhackers (verified): "Instead of writing your message, steal it." Bencivenga (verified, crediting Caples): "gather seven times more interesting information than I could possibly use." "Copy is not written, copy is assembled" is attributed to Schwartz and appears in no fetched source.

### 3.2 What to gather, in priority order of language quality

1. Interview and sales-call transcripts, raw (Elbaz, verified: "they relay their clues").
2. Open-ended survey answers, sorted longest first (Levesque's wording unfetched; the rule stands as practice).
3. Two-, three- and four-star reviews of the product, competitors, and books or products serving the same outcome. Wiebe (verified): "That's where the reality lives." Formula: `site:amazon.com inurl:"product-reviews" "tired of" ~keyword`. For SaaS, G2 and Capterra with the same tags; reviews naming three or more competitors signal stage 4-5.
4. Support tickets, chat logs, cancellation reasons.
5. Reddit and forum threads where prospects argue with each other.
6. Competitor promotions quoted exactly; the client's own winners and losers.
7. The product: features mapped to benefit chains, proof assets, mechanism, offer terms, refund rate, the first-week experience.

Review sites are post-purchase voice of customer. The rubric run found AG1's "replace my supplements" (the brand's long-running control) at two Trustpilot verbatims; pull at least one pre-purchase source (forums, sales-call notes, ad comments) or the minimum-count rule will demote the best argument. JTBD switch interviews (Rewired Group, verified): "A struggling moment is a point in time when you realize that something can be better"; ask why "not just once but several times"; "Everybody lies," so ask for a concrete recent example; ten interviews suffice.

### 3.3 Capture and tagging rules

One verbatim per record, exact wording, source and speaker role; "as few 'summaries' or abstractions as possible" (Beachway, verified); quotes in quotation marks, paraphrases unmarked. Tags: desire; frustration; prior solution and why it failed; existing belief; objection or anxiety; habit; trigger (Revella, verified: "What happened on the day you first decided you need to solve this kind of problem or achieve this goal?"); purchase criteria; proof need; sticky language; unexpected benefit; competitor mention; the desire cluster (from a cluster vocabulary fixed per market and logged); the matched intensity or duration marker. Rank themes by frequency and by length and specificity; three verbatims per theme; no theme without three exact quotes, and a confidence label where fewer exist.

### 3.4 Diagnosing awareness and sophistication

Awareness from traffic source, poll answers (product named → state 1-2; method or category → 3; symptom → 4), and whether the category word appears in the VOC. Sophistication from the competitor table, the logo-swap test, the qualifier/determiner sort, and exhausted-claim language. Moesta's forces (verified: push, pull, anxiety, habit): push-dominant means the lead crystallizes the problem; anxiety-dominant means the argument lives in proof and risk reversal; habit-dominant means the current solution must be made untenable first.

### 3.5 From dossier to three candidate arguments: the anchored rubric

Generate candidates by search first (the unused fact, the opposite idea, the mechanism in the process), then narrow. The gap-fill research anchored each criterion in the author's own test, defined 0-3 levels, computed the desire dimensions from VOC, and ran the rubric on AG1, Motley Fool and ClickUp with twenty candidates each. `04-copy-standard.md` Section 2 holds the anchors.

Two gates precede any sum. The desire gate: magnitude ≥4 of 9 with at least five verbatims (Schwartz: "If it is wrong, nothing else that you do in the ad will matter"). The proof gate: claim-to-proof ≥2, meaning the ledger holds a verifiable fact that covers the promise. Two taste criteria were replaced: "timeliness" became timing evidence (dated verbatims or a named event), and "the gasp" became Schwartz's second-sentence test run by a cold reader who sees only the headline and first sentence. Weights: desire dimensions ×2 each, claim-to-proof ×2, inevitable conclusion ×2, others ×1 (maximum 57). Tie-break: proof, then scope, then cold reader; still tied, present both.

Results: AG1's "Steady energy without coffee" (13 verbatims) failed the proof gate on testimonials only; "One 60-second habit that holds your routine together" won. Motley Fool's "Beat the market with a handful of picks" (29 verbatims) failed the proof gate; "Everything we know. One price. No upgrades." won, the only candidate where the claim and the proof are the same object, built against a competitor whose reviews were 17% upsell complaints. ClickUp's "Replace Slack, Notion, Asana, Loom" failed the desire gate; "Know what's slipping before your client does" won at 34-36 of 57, still promise-shaped, so the carrier must be visible in the lead.

Keep the two losers. Bencivenga (verified): "I'd much rather have in a good direct mail package three or four or 10 good reasons to buy, than to have to sacrifice nine of them in favor of the one USP." Losers become body reasons and the next two lead tests.

### 3.6 The proof hunt

Run against the three largest claims of each candidate before drafting, in order of strength: a demonstration the reader can run or watch; a result with a denominator from the client's own data; the full text of any study behind the mechanism (then dimensionalize: Schwalm's "2 log reduction means the number of germs is 100 times smaller"); process facts from the maker; named customers quotable with a specific and a town; the track record audited with its losses; the competitor comparison measured. Each find becomes a ledger row with its grade (Section 11.3). A claim whose hunt returns nothing is sized to what was found or replaced, never hedged.

### 3.7 What a research step must not do

Invent an avatar: Goff's "8-minute research" page (verified) asks ChatGPT for "the fears that might be going on in the head of a 55 year-old woman named Jane," returns "Fear of Becoming Invisible," cites no source and calls it "better than the research that 99% of copywriters would do." Label such output "unsourced hypothesis," outranked by any single verbatim. Smooth customer grammar. Rank by what the model finds persuasive. Describe competitors abstractly. Lead with category statistics.

## 4. Leads, headlines, hooks, bullets

### 4.1 Definitions

The *big idea* is the argument. The *hook* is the specific, surprising, true detail that makes it concrete (Carlton, verified: "the connecting point between the tangibles of the product... and the hazy inner needs of your prospect"; the one-legged golfer works because one leg matters to a swing). The *headline* is hook plus promise. The *deck* is the second headline (Ford, verified: "If it doesn't build on the excitement of the main headline, you have condemned the promotion"). The *lead* is the 300-500 words that pay the headline off and win commitment to read, ending at the first crosshead that names the mechanism or product.

### 4.2 Headline rules

Caples (secondary): self-interest first; "For every curiosity headline that succeed[s] in getting results, a dozen will fail." Hopkins (verified): "The purpose of a headline is to pick out people you can interest." Schwartz (verified): the headline's one job is the second sentence. Awareness reconciles them.

Believability and gradualization come before strength. Schwalm (verified): "Contrary to popular thinking, you can't always lead with your most powerful claim," and "the #1 rule to using gradualization effectively is to NOT violate your prospect's existing beliefs in the slightest"; her memory-supplement control put the 30-day speed claim at the end of the deck, because "If I had led with it, it may have immediately engendered disbelief." This resolves the first synthesis's conflict between Bly's "start your letter with your strongest sales point" and Schwartz: lead with the strongest claim the reader will accept without argument, then climb. Bencivenga (reported): "a reasonably easy requirement, followed by a strong promise"; a "moderately easy" requirement beats an "extremely easy" one.

Proof in the headline lifts response: Bencivenga reports "When Doctors Have Headaches, What Do They Do?" beat "Tension Headache?" by 71%; Caples' splits (verified): "RESPOND BY THURSDAY" pulled 39% more than the dated version; "IQ Test Enclosed" beat "Can You Afford A Dumb Worker?" by 138%.

Ledger-first rule: no institution, study, number, name or credential in a headline, deck or crosshead without a ledger ID. Goff's ChatGPT headline quiz (verified): all four "A-level" headlines were AI, two opened "Harvard Study Finds..." and "Yale Scientists Discover..." with no such study, and none carried a lexical tell. The ledger match is the detector; vocabulary is a tiebreaker.

Scoring: AWAI's 4 U's (definitions verified; the 1-4 scale not found in the 2001 article) only after the belief check; a reader of the article objected that "rebuilds new cartilage in less than 30 minutes" scores on every U and nobody believes it. Ford's inherent benefit (verified): give something the reader gets now by reading on ("See page 3, inside!").

### 4.3 The first fifty words and the cut-from-the-top pass

The WSJ letter (Conroy, 1975-2003) opens: "On a beautiful late spring afternoon, twenty-five years ago, two young men graduated from the same college." Halbert's openers (verified): "My name is Betty Adams and, the first thing you should know about me is... I am not a doctor." Checks: a concrete noun and a number or time in the first two sentences; an open question the reader cannot answer; the speaker identified by word 150 in one to three lines; no restatement of the headline. Carlton (verified): "we nearly always edit out the first and second pages" because first drafts are "clearing our throat." Delete opening paragraphs until the first sentence a stranger would keep reading. Belgray's version of this rule was labeled verified in the first synthesis, could not be located, and is dropped.

### 4.4 The lead-rewrite loop

1. Confirm the argument passed the rubric gates and has a hook from research.
2. Classify the reader by Schwartz's starting point (product, desire, problem, nothing) and pick the lead family plus one adjacent family.
3. Draft bullets, proof blocks, offer and close before the lead; the lead promises only what the close delivers.
4. Write three leads of 300-500 words on three reader hypotheses, each ending at a first crosshead (Georgi, verified: "about 90% of the time, the lead is actually the last thing I do").
5. Cut each from the top; reject any lead whose first 50 words contain no concrete noun, scene, number or named problem.
6. Cold read on a separate model with no brief (Section 10.2).
7. Pick the winner; mine the losers for crossheads (Schwalm swaps in a stronger hook found lower in the copy).
8. Rewrite the winner from scratch against the finished body (Deutsch: "the third time kind of does it right"); read aloud (Schwalm: "if you get out of breath reading it, that's a surefire sign"); judge every word as helping, hurting or neutral.
9. Write headline and deck last; belief check before the 4 U's; 25+ headlines in the family and 10 adjacent, starting with the plain how-to; hard-reject any that reaches maximum specificity while failing the ledger.
10. If the bullet pass produced a line more intriguing than the headline, promote it.

Pilot measurement: on AWAI's 5,766-word page the first crosshead falls at word 255 (4.4%); on an 8,016-word page at 125. Working spec: lead 300-600 words, 5-12% of a long promotion. The 20-control measurement remains open (swipe files host letters as images).

### 4.5 Bullets and fascinations

Mel Martin (verified via Kurtz): "What never to eat on an airplane," "Bills it's okay to pay late," "Vitamins never to buy in a health food store," "The two most forgotten tax deductions." Each is about something the reader already does, reverses an assumption, reads in a glance, and withholds the noun that closes the loop; none uses "discover," "unlock" or "secret to." Makepeace's newsletter (verified, by Tony Flores): Lampropoulos writes "as many as 700 fascinations" and keeps "about 100"; "even just one or two bad ones can cause your prospect to start 'skimming'"; "if everything is a secret then each one becomes a little less unique"; "How Doctors Stay Well While Treating Sick People All Day" began as a bullet and "hints of 'proof' because it references doctors." Bencivenga's standard: "How to end the agony of a toothache — with only two cloves!" Generate five to seven times the needed count, keep the top 15%, enforce variety (length; blind, half-blind, giveaway; type), promote the best proof-bearing bullet as a headline candidate.

### 4.6 Curiosity versus clarity

NextAfter (tested, lead-gen): "Get the Guide" 0.37% against "Detect Breast Cancer Earlier" 0.26% on completed forms. Robertson et al. 2023 (12,448 randomized Upworthy tests): each negative word raised click-through about 2.3%; clicks, not sales. Mad Mimi (verified): hiding a plan's price produced 927% more clicks and no additional sign-ups. Bencivenga's mystery premium out-pulled a described gift (his account). Rule: clarity when the prospect is product-aware or the next step is a form or purchase; curiosity for cold lists, subject lines and info products, only the kind that is a real situation plus a withheld specific and leads to the offer; never trade clarity about who it is for.

### 4.7 The turn

Where the lead becomes the pitch is where long copy loses readers and where AI reaches for "That's why we built..." (measured: 12% of AI drafts, 35% of AI emails, 0-1% of human controls). The 20-control measurement was not run; this is practice. The turn is a reason-why sentence that restates the mechanism as cause (the discovery: "The fix turned out to be the follow-up, not the agent. So we made the follow-up automatic."; the demonstration: "I was skeptical, so I ordered one on a Thursday"; Ford's false close with the "liquidating benefit," verified: "it liquidates the cost in the reader's mind"). The product is named where awareness allows: in the headline for state 1, at the turn for states 3-5. The first synthesis's "no product name in the first 15%" contradicted state 1 and is withdrawn.

### 4.8 Amateur and AI headline patterns with replacements

| Pattern | Replacement |
|---|---|
| "Unlock / Discover / Elevate / Transform your X" | Name the thing: "The 20 cent LIVER SAVER that fights off the damaging effects of alcohol..." |
| "Discover the secret to [generic benefit]" | A secret with a noun and provenance: "The 'Chaffee Royalty Program' That Turned Every $1 Into $50" |
| "The Ultimate Guide to X" | One result, one mechanism: "How Doctors Stay Well While Treating Sick People All Day" |
| Label:tagline colon headline | One sentence with a verb and a specific: "To men who want to quit work some day" |
| Question the reader answers instantly ("Want more sales?") | A question they cannot answer without reading: "Do You Make These Mistakes in English?" |
| "Harvard Study Finds..." with no study in the ledger | A ledger-backed authority with name, date and number, or no authority |
| "Stop [verb]ing" reflex (8% of AI drafts) | A proclamation with a date or a named fact |
| "In today's fast-paced world...", "Imagine a world where..." | Begin in the middle of a story or on the problem in few words |
| Hedged headline claim ("may help you potentially...") | The strongest claim the ledger supports; qualifiers in the proof section |
| "It's not about X. It's about Y." as the default opener | Allowed when it corrects a belief the reader holds (it is the shape of a problem-mechanism redefinition); rate-limited |

## 5. Structure by format

Bands are starting values calibrated against the library; "must" items are the reviewer's pass/fail checks; unsourced items are practitioner consensus or this document's synthesis. `04-copy-standard.md` Section 9 carries the one-page spec per format; this section carries the jobs, the evidence and the failures.

### 5.1 Long-form sales letter (3,000-8,000 words)

Agora's lead-body-close, Kennedy's 28 steps (titles verified), Georgi's RMBC order (verified) and Ford's eight stages (verified: Hook, Problem or Opportunity, Emotionally Compelling Idea, Solution or Offer, Proof, Overcoming Objections, False Close, Final Close) converge on one sequence. Headline and deck, graduated. Lead of 300-600 words: speaker by word 150, one claim a skeptic would doubt, no mechanism explained, product named only where awareness allows. Credibility in a few lines where the lead needs it; the first synthesis's 300-800-word background block manufactured the bio it warned against and is withdrawn. Problem mechanism of 300-600 words (Kennedy, verified: "problem, agitate, problem, invalidate all solutions except mine, and then the solution... It's extremely reliable"). Solution mechanism in two sentences, then its two proofs, then the turn. Product build-up and reveal, benefits argued as claim, reason, proof, picture (Schwartz: "Put the Claims in Action," "Show Him How to Test Your Claims," "Picture the Black Side, Too"). Proof block as reinforcement, never first appearance. Offer and stack; price once with reframe; denominated guarantee; urgency with reason; close at 15-25% with the CTA at least three times and what happens after the click; a P.S. carrying whatever the skimmer most needs; FAQ from research only. The skim path (headline, deck, crossheads, bold, captions, P.S., order form) must sell alone; Kennedy's "double readership path" is the source (step title verified). Failures: uniform section lengths; benefits listed; proof wall at the end; no mechanism; summary close; a bio before the reader knows why to care; a turn with no cause; dateless urgency.

### 5.2 Short landing page (150-600 words; product-aware traffic)

Headline matched to the ad promise; subhead naming who it is for and the differentiator; CTA with the action in the button; three to five argued benefits with proof in the line; one proof strip; two objections with proof and the guarantee; repeated CTA. B2B demo and lead-gen pages: future-pace the next step (CRE Earth Class Mail, tested, +61% leads with "Here's what you'll get"); hard and soft offer paths (Bly); indicate price (NN/g: pricing ranks first of 28 information types B2B users want); SaaS band 300-750 words (Unbounce). Failure: problem education for people who know the product.

### 5.3 Opt-in page (60-250 words)

Headline stating the outcome with a number, timeframe or named mechanism (Copy Posse's 79% page, self-reported: "100 subject lines that drive more clicks and engagement"); three to six specific, incomplete bullets; email-only form (ClickFunnels, verified: "each additional form field will decrease the conversion rate"); a button that completes "I want..."; one proof line. Failures: a headline that describes the artifact; bullets as a table of contents; "Submit."

### 5.4 Upsell / OTO page (300-900 words or a 3-5 minute video)

Brunson (Goodreads highlights): "Rule #1: Don't Sell More of the Same Thing. Rule #2: Don't Sell a Random Product." SamCart (vendor): take rates fall from 43% for a $17 bump on $47 to about 1% at $1,997. Spec: confirm the first decision in one line; the bridge to the next problem the purchase creates; the offer as the shortest path, mechanism in a sentence; the true reason it is only here; stack and price; one-line guarantee; a Yes button stating the benefit; a plain No (SamCart, verified: no guilt declines, no resetting timers). Failures: re-selling the first product; "disappears forever"; three sentences of congratulations first.

### 5.5 Order bump (30-80 words above the payment button)

Checkbox; bolded "Yes! Add [thing] for only $X"; two or three sentences (what it is, how it makes the main purchase work faster, why only here if true). SamCart (vendor): 20-40% of the main price, "convert at 30-40% on average"; a same-priced bump "feels like a second purchase decision."

### 5.6 Order form and checkout (new)

Written first (Christensen via Forde, verified) and read first. Required: a one-line acceptance statement; the recap in the same words as the close (Ingram via Bly, verified: "Add an offer summary box. Stress the deadline."); price, total and every added cost (Baymard, tested: 40% of non-browsing abandoners cite added costs); what arrives and when; the guarantee restated verbatim beside the button (CRE Geeks2U, tested: three successive rewrites, +11%, +21%, +24%, 49% cumulative); the deadline as a fact (Bencivenga: "Any orders received thereafter will be reluctantly returned"); a trust block in the payment area (Baymard: "this is about perceived security of the fields"); one testimonial with photo beside the button about the buying experience (Forde, verified; Express Watches, tested: a Trustpilot widget below Add to Basket, +58.29%). Carlton (verified): list the highest-priced option first; "Letting him see the lowest price first gives him an 'out.'" Baymard: 18% abandon on forced account creation. Must not introduce any bonus, price, tier or claim the close did not state, re-sell at length (about 120 words outside the recap), or say "Submit." The consistency check, run in factual review with the offer block as source of truth across close, order form and every email: product and tier names; deliverables and bonuses; price, plan, currency, strikethrough; added costs; the guarantee sentence; deadline and reason; delivery promise; button verb.

### 5.7 Thank-you page (80-250 words)

Confirmation with where the thing is; one instruction for the next sixty seconds; a bridge to one low-ticket offer tied to the magnet's promise; one social action at most. Copyhackers' 52% and 39% lifts are the author's own unsourced results.

### 5.8 Single email (30-80 words for a cart-close; 200-350 for an objection email; 350-700 for a story email)

One reader (Belgray, verified: "Write to ONE PERSON"), one idea (Schwalm, verified: "stick with one 'big idea' in your email... and run through it in a logical progression"), one destination; the job is the click or reply. Structure (Settle's posts, verified): hook line; one story or argument; a one-sentence bridge ("More about the paid Email Players newsletter here:"); one link; sign-off; P.S. past about 250 words. Ford (verified): "begin your conversation with them by scrupulously NOT selling it"; delete the story and if the pitch still reads fine, the story was decoration.

Three items the first synthesis stated as rules are voice parameters or one practitioner's taste. Greeting: Belgray (verified) says "A formal greeting signals 'we're not friends,'" and Halbert opened "Dear Friend"; it belongs in the voice profile. Questions and bullets: Throssell's Compendium advises against both in sales emails; Sackheim's question headline ran forty years and Agora emails are built on fascinations, so neither is a failure flag. Subject line written after the body, the most specific interesting line pulled from it (Belgray, verified); GetResponse (correlational): under 70 characters, non-personalized out-opened personalized. HubSpot's own A/B series (verified): "In every single A/B test, the simpler-designed email won." Failures: "I hope this finds you well"; subject restated as line one; rhetorical pain question; three benefits with "so you can"; multiple links; "That's why we built" (35% of AI emails); Belgray's banned beats (verified): "'Not this. That.' constructions," "Overly tidy 'rule of three' punchline patterns," "Let that sink in," "Anything that sounds like it's trying to be quotable."

### 5.9 Sequences

Every non-final email ends with a concrete reason to open the next; the reviewer checks that N's loop is addressed in N+1. Welcome (5-7 emails): deliver and reframe; origin story with the lesson as the product's premise; belief shift; proof story; objection email (Belgray, verified: "Every question about your product is an objection"); direct offer; Throssell (verified) runs at least ten and sells in all. Engagement (ClickFunnels' reproduction of the Soap Opera Sequence): "Each email is written to pull the reader toward the next email." Launch (10-12): three prelaunch, open-cart, four mid-cart (biggest objection with proof, customer story, FAQ, who this is not for), three close-day (Orzechowski, verified: morning, one hour before, 15 minutes before), post-close; "half of sales in the last 24 hours" is lore. Abandoned cart (Omnisend, vendor): 30-60 minutes, 24 hours, 48-72 hours; no discount first. Post-purchase (Klaviyo, verified: opens "almost 17% higher than the average email automation"): fast win, how to get the result, first-win check-in, review request after use, next offer with a deadline. Win-back: Dean Jackson's nine-word email (verified), "Are you still interested in [what they opted in for]?", no links.

### 5.10 Webinar package (corrected against Expert Secrets, One to Many and the transcripts)

A pitch presentation is not a lesson. Brunson (transcript, verified): his best teaching presentation sold nothing; the fix he credits to Kennedy and Glazer is that the only job is to give belief that the product can help. Fladlien (One to Many, verified) reverses the first synthesis's "offer is 80%": "the offer is the least important part of the close. Most presenters think it's the most important"; he spends "about three to five minutes" on it, and his 80/20 line is about the customer's effort-to-result ratio.

Registration: the One Thing phrased so it cannot be answered in the reader's head; the three secrets as bullets. Timing (Expert Secrets, verified): "The webinar should be about 90 minutes long. The first 60 minutes, you focus on breaking and rebuilding their false belief patterns... The last 30 minutes is the pitch... I usually spend the remaining 15–30 minutes for Q&A, closing people between each question." Drop-offs (transcript): "the first 10 minutes and... right when you transition to your close." Every finer minute allocation in the first synthesis was invented and is withdrawn; the only measured marks are Brunson's walkthrough of his own slides (transition 47:09-50:58; stack 50:58-57:31; closes 69:35-72:20), not a live control.

Beats (Expert Secrets slide list, verified): title; rapport; Ruler Goal #1 (the new opportunity); Ruler Goal #2 (the Big Domino); qualify yourself; Epiphany Bridge #1; liken your story to them; case study; transition to three secrets; per secret, state it, Epiphany Bridge, others' results, break related beliefs, restate the new belief ("how to [want] without [thing they hate]"). Fladlien's intro (verified): "five testimonials in the first five minutes"; get them "in writing, to... commit to something major"; and the two-agendas frame, verbatim: "I have two agendas here today. My first agenda is to teach you X Y and Z. My second agenda is to sell you something. Now, if I don't make good on teaching you X, Y, and Z, then I don't want you to buy... However, by some small miracle, if I'm able to actually show you X, Y, and Z, then will you at least consider the offer...?" Belief triage (transcript, paraphrase; he never says "bucket"): what they will never accept, what they accept now, what they could accept under the right circumstances; build on the third.

Transition (verified): "Let me ask you a question," then the permission ask; Fladlien's "sixty minutes in sixty seconds," the "half-dozen yeses," "The Two Choices," then the product name. Stack (Expert Secrets Secret #15, verified): show each element, add it to the stack slide, re-show the full stack; If-All statements; price drop; price reveal as the first CTA, carried on every slide after; justification; guarantee; restack; urgency bonus; closing slide with recap, price, URL, countdown. Fladlien's close order (verified, "nine times out of ten"): Offer (under three minutes means the offer is too weak) → Price (stair-step from a real higher reference) → Bonuses (second-best first, best last) → Scarcity throughout → Guarantee → Objections cycled (price, time, belief, product, back to price "a different way"). Reveal price "within the first five minutes of introducing the offer" because "they are likely to say no the first time anyway"; "I aim for twenty-six times." The "give me a no upfront" line is Preston paraphrasing Fladlien. Closes: sixteen named in Expert Secrets; "seven or eight or nine" per webinar now (transcript); trial closes lifted earnings from about $9 to over $16 per registrant (his figure). Countdown (transcript): 10- and 15-minute clocks stopped sales at zero; with 30 minutes and live Q&A "probably three-fourths of my sales come during this... 30 minute countdown." Hormozi's checklist (verified): "Always match the guarantee terms with the activation points in your program." Replay (WebinarJam, verified): "A deadline you extend once is a deadline nobody believes again." Failures: teaching to completeness; secrets that are tips; apology transition; feature list then price; one objection for fifteen minutes; invented scarcity; default "30-day money back"; a twenty-minute origin story.

### 5.11 VSL, long (warm or retargeted traffic)

Benson (verified): VSLs "live and die" by "the introduction, which can be as short as 7 seconds and as long as maybe a minute, and the pitch"; changing "the first 500 words" alone produced "massive increases" (no numbers); the page is a headline, the video and a buy button that drops when the price is stated. His step names (channel titles; definitions unverified): Snap Suggestion, Vital Connection, Big Problem, Bigger Solution, Grand Offer. Milligan's reading of Project Prophecy (one practitioner): pattern interrupt ("I'm holding an unauthorized version of a classified government document in my hand"), big promise boxed with mechanism ("walk away with an average of $15,760 every two weeks"), a promise that deliberately triggers the BS reaction, immediate dated demonstration ("$500 into $10,000"), the how withheld until just before the pitch. Beats: interrupt; promise with mechanism; credibility and bribe; dated demonstration; catalyst; short discovery story; the plan with logic per step; who it is for and objections as personas; transition and stack, button drops; price, guarantee, urgency, CTA repeated. Minute ranges are withdrawn; the control VSLs were not time-stamped. Failures: biography first; "our proven system"; price before value.

### 5.12 VSL, short (3-7 minutes, cold traffic)

This document's construction, consistent with Benson's description of his five-minute VSL as building credibility and anticipation for the next step: interrupt tied to the promise; promise, mechanism name and credibility in one breath; catalyst; three fascination-style benefits or a mini-demonstration; who it is for plus one disqualifier; the next step and what happens after the click; one real reason to act now. No stack, no price unless the page sells.

### 5.13 Meta and Instagram ads

Specs (verified): Facebook Feed 50-150 characters of primary text, 27-character headline; Instagram 125 characters, 40-character headline. Hook (call-out, implied outcome, open loop), one angle, one proof, one literal CTA; the headline field restates the offer. Structured (verified): "every ad set is a theory," 3-6 variations per set. UGC copy: lowercase, no brand voice, a sentence a customer would type. Policy (verified): "ads must not contain content that asserts or implies personal attributes"; "Do you have diabetes?" banned, "New diabetes treatment available" allowed: describe the product or outcome, never the reader. Failures: "Are you tired of..."; "to the next level"; emoji bullets (95% of AI ad drafts); "Learn more today!"; the mechanism explained in full.

### 5.14 YouTube ad scripts

Specs (verified): skippable after 5 seconds; a view counts at 10 seconds. 0-5s call-out and disqualifier; 5-15s stakes and one credibility line; 15-45s mechanism; 45s+ proof, offer, spoken and on-screen CTA, rehook, second CTA. Cut the logo intro and "At [Brand], we." Policy (verified): no "claims that entice the user with an improbable result (even if this result is possible)," no "death, accidents, illness, arrests or bankruptcy to induce fear, guilt."

### 5.15 TikTok and Reels (15-45 seconds, under about 90 spoken words)

TikTok (verified): "Introduce your content proposition in the first 3 seconds," "5-10 words per second when using text," feature "creators, employees, or customers." Line one spoken and captioned (result-first, contrarian, POV); lines two to four show the discovery; one proof; CTA with location. Failure: "Hey everyone!", a feature tour.

### 5.16 Google responsive search ads

Specs (verified): up to 15 headlines of 30 characters, 4 descriptions of 90; pins to Headline 1, 2 or Description 1 always show. Quota: 3-4 keyword headlines, 3-4 benefit-with-number, 2-3 proof, 2 offer or risk-reversal, 2 CTA; each description a complete argument with its CTA. Failure: headlines of one shape.

### 5.17 Advertorial and native landing page (800-1,800 words cold; 400-700 retargeting)

Halbert's "Million Dollar Smile" (Schauer's annotation) runs an editorial lead, a 16-level whiteness chart compared to diamond grading, objections on pain and cost and a testimonial close, in a customer's voice. Halbert (verified): an ad "should look, in so far as possible, exactly like a rave review written by a reporter." Spec: FTC label above the headline ("Ad," "Advertisement," "Paid Advertisement"; "Promoted" is "at best ambiguous"); editorial headline; byline and date; scene or surprising fact, no pitch for 150-300 words; the usual fixes widened; mechanism explained simply; proof with ledger names; the turn in the same voice; offer, price, guarantee, scarcity reason, links repeated in the last third. Listicle form: "5 Reasons Nurses Are Switching To This $40 Compression Sock," reasons escalating problem to mechanism to proof to offer. Outbrain (verified) bans "stuns," "reveals," "real reason," "you won't believe," and states "AI shall not be used to fabricate testimonials, simulate user reviews, generate unsubstantiated product claims, or produce synthetic 'before and after' imagery"; an invented narrator is a fabricated testimonial. Failures: brand named before word 250 (this document's number); opening with a summary; "Click here to buy now!" as the turn; no label.

### 5.18 Organic social

Ship 30 for 30 (verified): "You get 5 lines to hook the reader and get them to click 'see more'"; Lead-In, Main Points, CTA; link in a comment. Hook, then rehook ("College completely failed in teaching me how to write." / "But unlike college, these won't cost you $120,000."). Throssell (verified): "almost every single email I send is a sales email, too"; every post carries a sales intent inside a story or opinion. Failures: three ideas and a "Thoughts?" tail; emoji as bullets.

### 5.19 Product description (60-150 words of sell copy for consumables; 150-400 for considered purchases)

Copyhackers (verified, Wiebe's 2013 teardown): one goal per page, emotion then logic; "brief but high quality copy" plus "factual bullet points" is a "conversion slayer." Shopify's example: Dr. Squatch, "Made with real pine extract, this all-star bar is as tough as a freshly cut bat." Spec: name plus benefit tag; a one or two sentence lead in the buyer's words; three to five bullets translating features to situations; proof line; specs in a separate block; objection pre-empt. Generate from the review corpus and check every quoted phrase against it. Amazon's bullet rules must be pulled from Seller Central before use. Failures: "Elevate your [category] experience," "crafted with care," superlatives without the proof line.

### 5.20 SMS (75-115 characters; never above 240)

Attentive's propensity-matched analysis of 25 billion messages (verified, the strongest evidence here for any short format): over 240 characters converted 8.5% worse; scarcity or urgency +12% CVR, percentage-off +10% over dollar-off, second person +9%, humor -11.4%, jargon -9.8%, generalization -8.2%; scarcity plus negative framing +24.5%. High performer: "Our best sale of the season starts NOW and you don't want to miss it. Get 25% off sitewide this weekend only. Shop now." Spec: [Brand]: [one-idea hook in second person with a stock, scarcity or newness fact] [offer with % and deadline] [link]. Compliance: CTIA (verified): "Standardized 'STOP' wording should be used"; TCPA (verified): no texts before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. recipient's time, "prior express written consent," revocation honored within ten business days. Failures: 200-300 characters, jokes, "we're excited to announce," dollar-off, no deadline, invented "almost gone."

### 5.21 Cold email (40-90 words; no links in email one)

Berman (verified, no data): "If your email is longer than 75 words, cut it."; "I noticed [specific observation about their company]." / "We help [their type of company] [specific outcome] without [common pain point]." / "Worth a quick call to see if this makes sense for [company name]?" Copyhackers (Lopuch, verified): "Being relevant is the secret sauce that makes a cold email feel warm." Buchan (verified): "You've never heard of me. (Hi, I'm Jon). I got your details from a list. gasp."; "Your job is to sell the idea that a call or meeting with you is not a bad idea." Gong (28M emails): pitching cuts replies up to 57%; an offer-based CTA beats a meeting ask. Lavender (231,818 emails): "Technical jargon turns engineers away"; finance needs "the logic chain, not the logo"; tentative phrasing ("I could be wrong, but...") out-replies assumptive. Spec: line one a verifiable observation; line two the implied problem or a one-line case study with a number; line three one question answerable in a word; follow-ups one to three sentences with one new piece of relevance. Failures: "I hope this email finds you well"; "I came across your profile and was impressed"; "I'd love to schedule a 30-minute call"; a case study with no number.

### 5.22 B2B pricing page (new)

Value headline (Copyhackers' example: "Cheaper than downtime."); plans as "Best for [persona]"; an offer that neutralizes a researched objection; proof at the decision point; FAQ headings phrased as the objection; three plans (CXL: RingCentral 4→3 plans +37%); anchor against the expensive alternative (CRE Smart Insights: +157%); restate the value argument above the price (CRE Earth Class Mail +57%). Wynter (n=50): 58% never tested the page.

### 5.23 Podcast and audio ad reads

Spotify (verified) for produced spots: 55-70 words; brand name within five seconds; takeaways "from least to most important"; one CTA. Host reads: talking points, not a script; a "why I use it" moment; one mechanism; one proof; one objection; code and URL said twice, last.

Formats still without a spec (downsell, application and call-booking page, quiz funnel, webinar invitation emails, cancellation flow, UGC video script, X thread, replay page) are open items in Section 14.

## 6. Proof and belief

### 6.1 The governing law

Bencivenga (verified): "Yeah, sure" is "the two most powerful and influential words in advertising today... mass murderers of response"; response comes "not by cranking up the volume on your promise... It is by raising the level of your proof." Schwartz (verified): the reader demands "Tell me more," "Oh yeah? Who says so?" and "How does it work?", and "you must answer these questions or your copy will fail."

### 6.2 Taxonomy

| Type | What it is | Strength | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Demonstration | Reader sees or performs the result (BluBlocker: "When I put on the pair of glasses what I saw I could not believe. Nor will you."; Schwartz's "4,076 roses all from one single plant") | Highest | Lead or first promise; VSL first third |
| Specific result with denominator | "9 of 14 beta users...", "52.7%" | High | Adjacent to the headline claim |
| Mechanism (reason-why) | Causal "because" ("Kleenex towels absorb 50 percent more because they're two layers thick") | High; required at stage 3+ | After the promise sounds too good |
| Process specifics | Counts, iterations, hours ("130 formulas," "78-second shave") | High, free | Body; new products |
| Named authority | Person, title, publication, test | High if checkable, zero without a ledger ID | Stacked after a comparison |
| Documented testimonial | Name, place, number, time, an imperfection | Medium-high | Beside the claim; on the order form, about the buying experience |
| Candor / damaging admission | True minor flaw next to the claim | Multiplier | Just before the strongest claim |
| Social proof volume | "12,418 customers" (exact) | Medium if exact; near zero if vague | Never alone |
| Generic testimonial; "trusted by thousands," "studies show," adjective stacks | No carrier | Negative | Cut |

B2B proof is persona-dependent. Bly (verified): engineers "want to know the features and specifications, not just the benefits" and "are not turned off by jargon—in fact, they like it"; nontechies want "benefits, business results, and the reputation and credibility of the vendor." Lavender: engineering responds to deployment time and incident reduction; finance to the cost chain; ops and marketing to explicit customer proof.

### 6.3 Placement

Schwartz (verified): "the placement of your proof is as important to its overall effect as the content itself"; documentation "is inherently dull... You have to stage it." Gradualization: "a statement that will be immediately and entirely accepted, and then building a chain of subsequent acceptances." Mechanization: name, describe (Rinso's "the suds that float off dirt" arrives when the promise "begins to sound too good to be true"), feature in the headline; "it is not scientific discourse... You must load it with promise, load it with emotion." By format: sales page, a hint of proof in the headline, first proof at the first big promise, each claim followed by its carrier, admission before the strongest claim, guarantee after price; order form, the guarantee and one experience testimonial by the button; email, one claim and one carrier; VSL and webinar, demonstration in the first third; ads, the proof is the headline. No public test isolates distributed from clustered testimonials, so distribution is a default, not a gate.

### 6.4 The damaging admission

Caples (verified): "admitting that there are some weak points as well as strong points," with Bedell's "The Guest House sags at the seams, but perhaps You can make it livable... But the potential is great, and the price downright tempting." Schwartz (verified) on "He is not an accomplished writer, and it has flaws": the frankness "serves as an intensifying contrast to the positive claims of the second paragraph." One admission per piece, true, minor, immediately followed by the claim it licenses, from the brief, never a hedge ("Four of the 31 people in the beta did nothing with it and got nothing" is candor; "Results may vary" is not).

### 6.5 Testimonials and new products

Caples (verified): "Providing a testimonial is true and can be documented." Forde (verified) on failures: "emotionally unsatisfying and vague," "too gushy," "too polished," "a legal risk or just plain fake." Bly (verified): "specific, detailed testimonials," "not edited for style," full attribution. A testimonial is proof only with a specific, placed beside its claim, never generated. For new products, in order: process specifics; mechanism as cause; the maker's own logged result including what failed; named science at the ledger's grade; a demonstration; a beta cohort with the denominator; risk reversal that costs the seller (Hopkins, verified: "Pay in a week if you like them" proved "many times as impressive"); candor about who it is not for.

### 6.6 Failure patterns

Generic social proof; adjectives in place of evidence; proof wall at the end; hedges as substitute for proof; invented authorities ("Harvard Study Finds"); mechanism missing; claim bigger than proof; rounding away the proof; disclaimers mistaken for candor; visualization before belief; the guarantee doing all the proving; a lead with nothing to disbelieve.

## 7. Offer construction and presentation

### 7.1 What an offer is

Kobs (via AWAI): "whatever you physically get in the package or carton, plus the financial arrangement and any 'strings' or commitments"; "Don't confuse your offer with your sales close." His 99 offers (categories verified via snippets) run from basic terms through free gifts, discounts, time limits, guarantees, build-up-the-sale and continuity to specialized devices. Joyner's Big Four (via a reviewer): "What are you trying to sell me?", "How much?", "Why should I believe you?", "What's in it for me?" Hormozi's value equation (via notes) is a thinking tool: dream outcome × perceived likelihood ÷ (time delay × effort).

### 7.2 The offer options sheet

For each variant: when used, front-end effect, back-end effect, what the copy must say. Hard cash with order: low-ticket, cold traffic; baseline 100. Bill-me and installments: high-ticket, info, B2B; +50% to +130% (Benson, Stone); Kobs: "Terms usually produce an even better response than 'bill me.'" Free trial: subscriptions, software; roughly 1.5-2x hard (Goss: 48% against 25%); count paid-ups (Stone: "Fortunes have been lost by counting trial orders instead of counting paid-ups"). Money-back: default for cash; longer outpulls shorter (Bly's splits: 60-day beat 30-day; "free use" beat "free trial" by 15%). Premium-led: editorial premiums beat hard goods for information buyers (Kurtz's Tax Hotline test). Continuity: take rate at least 2:1 over positive option (Hatch, Stone); ROSCA (verified): material terms "before obtaining the consumer's billing information," express consent, "simple mechanisms" to cancel. Then Abraham's flashpoint audit (verified), each fix a pending ledger entry with client approval. If no true urgency mechanism exists, propose one (bonus expiry, price-rise date, cohort start, capacity cap) or use a lead-generation offer.

### 7.3 Presentation sequence

1. Transition that names the thing.
2. The core offer, named with the outcome and interval, shown worth the price alone.
3. The value frame.
4. Bonuses, each the answer to a named worry, with a dollar value only when the ledger holds a basis; Hormozi (via notes): "Never discount core deal; present bonuses instead."
5. The price, once, then the reframe. Gourville (*JCR* 1998, tested): pennies-a-day framing works when the comparison is comparable. Price-drop rungs must be real reference prices. No apology: "$497. That's less than one lost client. If it doesn't bring you two in 60 days, you pay nothing."
6. The guarantee, denominated and named. Abraham (verified) replaces "Your satisfaction is guaranteed" with a 60-day supplement guarantee listing expected effects and "If that doesn't start occurring before the 60th day of diligently using this regimen, you are fully entitled to a complete refund"; "Don't sabotage a great risk-reversal offer by adding a lot of qualifiers or conditions." His lift figures are anecdotes.
7. The reason to act now, with its mechanism.
8. The CTA with high sidewalls (Kennedy, verified: "Most marketers' failures and disappointments result from giving confusing directions or no directions at all").
9. Objections after the first CTA, each with proof and the restated guarantee.
10. Second CTA and P.S.

Compression: an email carries 1, 2 in one line, 5 or 7, 8 and a P.S.; an ad carries 2 and 6 or 7; a webinar carries 2-8 as the stack with trial closes; the order form carries 2, 5, 6, 7 and 8 verbatim.

### 7.4 Checklist and failures

Construction: variant chosen from the sheet with its reason; the first visible win and its interval named; the biggest reason-they'd-fail aimed at by a component or guarantee; every component traced to a researched problem; the bundle uncomparable; the promise checked against the first-week experience and refund window. Presentation: Big Four in order; core worth the price before bonuses; price once, no apology; every value figure and rung ledger-backed; guarantee denominated with a reason; deadline with mechanism; one CTA path; offer identical at close, checkout and in every email. Failures: feature list as offer; fabricated stack values; apologetic pricing; generic bolt-on guarantee; bonuses as padding; dateless urgency; evergreen timers by default; guilt-trip declines; two things sold at once (Benson); an order form that states a different deal from the close.

## 8. Story, emotion and voice

### 8.1 What story is for

Story delivers an argument a direct claim would bounce off. Halbert (verified): "YOU CAN DO A BETTER SELLING JOB WHEN AT FIRST IT DOES NOT APPEAR YOU ARE ATTEMPTING TO DO A SALES JOB." Forde (verified): sales stories are "usually short, just long enough to illustrate an idea," carrying "a proof or promise." The beat test is Parker and Stone's, relayed by Forde: "Every 'and then' is a moment where you could lose your reader"; beats are joined by "therefore" or "but," and AI drafts are "and then" chains. Per format: sales page and VSL lead, a scene with the big idea inside (Forde's International Living lead, verified: "You look out your window, past your gardener, who is busily pruning the lemon, cherry, and fig trees..."); email, one story, one point, one link; webinar, one origin story plus one per false belief; ads, one scene or identification line; opt-in pages, order forms and upsells, none.

Story policy: a story is (a) the client's documented story mapped to ledger rows, (b) an explicitly framed second-person hypothetical ("Say you..."), or (c) a sourced public anecdote. No fourth category. A composite customer or an invented "I was skeptical, so I ordered one" narrator is a fabricated testimonial (16 CFR 465; Outbrain). The ledger carries story-claim rows: biographical claim, customer result inside a narrative, dated event, named third party.

### 8.2 Emotional drivers

Makepeace (verified): features "are a yawn because they're about the product; not about the prospect"; "Get Off the Hormone Roller Coaster!" is a faux benefit, the real one is stopping hot flashes, and the emotion underneath is the threat to "the intimacy and security of their primary relationships." Find the one emotion already resident in the market's language, name it, agree with it before promising. The phrase "dominant resident emotion" is widely attributed to Makepeace's materials; the fetched article uses "resident" and "dominant" separately, and the compound was not verified. B2B keeps the rule: the buyer spending company money buys on status and approval dressed as ROI, with fear of looking incompetent to a boss or client as the resident emotion; humor is off unless the ledger holds a bold guarantee (Marshall, verified: "if you can't make an outrageous guarantee, then your outrageous advertising is disrespectful"). Forde (verified): sensory details "appeal most to emotions, the latter [numbers] to logic," so scenes go in lead and close, numbers in proof. Halbert (verified): "a bushel of money" beats "dramatically increase your sales volume."

### 8.3 Identification

Godin (verified): "we are driven to become a member in good standing of the tribe." One to three sentences of status-matching ("I am not a doctor"). Chaperon (verified): "Harper needs a guide, not a hero to emulate." A common enemy is external and already resented. Brunson's Epiphany Bridge (Expert Secrets, verified) runs Backstory, Desires, The Wall, The Epiphany, The Plan, The Conflict, The Achievement, The Transformation.

### 8.4 Voice and one readability metric

Flesch (verified): "Conversational English for consumers should score at least 80" (Reading Ease). Ford (verified): "An FK score of 7.0 to 8.5 is my goal" (grade). The first synthesis cited both as one scale. The engine uses Flesch-Kincaid grade only, as a flag, never scored. Bands (Unbounce, Bly, Lavender): consumer grade 5-8; B2B marketing, ops and executive copy 6-8; finance and legal 9-12 allowed ("Financial services is the only industry where more complex language can actually help conversions"); engineering, plain syntax with precise nouns. The voice sample overrides any band.

Ogilvy's 1982 memo (verified): "Write the way you talk. Naturally."; "Read it aloud the next morning—and then edit it." Carlton (verified): "With a gun to my head that would go off if the piece didn't become the control... would I use that word?" The measured corpus found sentence-length variance does not separate AI from human DR; human DR is louder (exclamation marks 6.1 per thousand words against 0.25; ellipses 9.7 against 0.5; fragment rows in 97% of Halbert issues). A detector that penalizes caps, fragments or exclamation marks fails the human control corpus.

### 8.5 Voice-capture procedure

From three to ten samples: sentence-length mean and deviation, share under six and over thirty words, fragment rate, FK grade; paragraph habits and openers; greeting and sign-off; an idiom bank of 20-40 phrases and a dead-word list; humor type and rate; profanity and edge tolerance; formatting habits; pronoun ratio; stance markers; story-opening habit; three verbatim exemplar paragraphs. Chaperon (verified): Grammarly "fought my voice, constantly trying to formalize and flatten my writing." Validate by regenerating and re-measuring. Usborne (verified): voice "is a mask. And masks can be copied"; what cannot be copied is lived story, so pair the profile with a story inventory.

### 8.6 Selling voice versus advising voice

Selling: direct address by the second sentence; a speaker with a name, a stake and an opinion by word 150; claims in the indicative; questions asked and answered; costs of inaction as scenes; a close that says exactly what to do. Advising, to reject: "Whether you're a... or a..." openers; "Here's why that matters:"; "Let's break it down"; both-sides framing; objections the market never raised; cheerful closers; "we/our" in place of a named "I"; "You're not alone" and "If that sounds familiar" (measured: present only in AI drafts).

## 9. The AI failure taxonomy

### 9.1 What the evidence says

No public controlled test compares AI-written and human-written long-form DR copy on conversions. Zhang & Gosline (*Judgment and Decision Making* 2023, N=1,203): blind, GPT-4's sub-100-word marketing copy out-scored ten consultants' on satisfaction and willingness-to-pay, and being told a piece was human-made raised ratings ("human favoritism rather than AI aversion"). Meguellati et al. (arXiv 2512.03373, preprint): when a participant correctly identified an ad as AI, preference fell 21.2 points. Goldstein et al. (*PNAS Nexus* 2024, N=8,221): the comparators were covert-campaign propaganda articles and the outcome was agreement with a thesis; uncurated GPT-3 reached 43.5% against 47.4%, prompt editing plus best-of-three 52.7%. The first synthesis read this as "best-of-N beat professionals"; it did not. Jakesch, Hancock & Naaman (*PNAS* 2023, N=4,600): people cannot detect AI text and rely on flawed cues.

The operational reading is this document's synthesis: readers penalize text they recognize as AI and text that is generic; neither study measured sales; the fixes live in the argument and the proof. Goff's headline quiz (verified) is the sharper lesson: four AI headlines with zero lexical tells, two unmailable because the university study they cite does not exist. The ledger match is the primary detector; vocabulary is a tiebreaker.

### 9.2 What was measured

This project generated 107 plain-brief drafts across six formats (sales-page lead, email, Meta ad, opt-in, upsell, VSL opener) with three Claude models and compared them to 341 human DR documents (157 Halbert newsletters, 68 Settle emails, 95 Belgray posts, 21 Carlton posts; 653,000 words); the scripts, the 107 AI drafts and the aggregate results are in `research/tells/` in this repo; the human corpus is not committed because the newsletters and posts are copyrighted. Limits: Claude-only on the AI side; no financial controls or Agora emails on the human side.

Three first-synthesis detectors failed. The GPT-4o-era phrase list ("game-changer," "Picture this," "Here's the kicker") occurred zero times in 107 drafts. Wikipedia's era vocabulary hit 3% of AI drafts against 20-43% of human documents. Negative parallelism ("not just X but Y"; "It's not X. It's Y.") appeared in 0% and 2% of AI drafts against 10-18% and 3% of human DR, so the zero-instances rule is withdrawn and the construction is rate-limited. Em dashes ran 1.57 per thousand words in human DR against 0.70 in AI; the cap is withdrawn. Sentence-length variance does not separate the corpora.

### 9.3 Lexical and structural tiers

Tier 0, hard reject: assistant residue; bracket placeholders; the word "AI" in copy for a non-AI product; any institution, study, number, name or credential with no ledger ID; bold-label-colon bullets, markdown headings or emoji bullets in email, sales page or VSL. Tier 1 (weight 4) holds the measured phrase tells from the table above plus summary closers and self-posed question headers; Tier 2 (weight 2) holds density tells (rule of three above 3 per thousand words, trailing participles above 0.6, hedges above 1, "rather than" above 2 per 300 words, generic emotion words as the only emotional content, question or "Imagine" openers not answered by a specific); Tier 3 (weight 1, never alone) holds the 4o-era list and the era vocabulary. Score = sum of weights per 300 words; review flag at 6, hard fail at 10 or any Tier 0 hit. Cadence is not a discriminator; a per-format floor applies instead (flag a sentence-length coefficient of variation under 0.70 in 300+ words). `04-copy-standard.md` Section 7 carries each tell with an example, a fix and the per-format overrides.

### 9.4 Argumentative tells

These cost sales when the prose is clean: no central argument; wrong awareness level; feature-by-feature benefits with "so you can"; no mechanism; proof dumped at the bottom or absent; invented objections and tidy FAQs; generic specificity ("over 10 years," "award-winning," "up to 50%"); hedged claims the ledger supports; the obvious angle (fix by searching the research for the unused fact); promise inflation; installed emotion with no verbatim behind it; prevention framing (Hopkins, verified: "People will do much to cure trouble, but people in general will do little to prevent it"); a lead with nothing to disbelieve; a headline promise never paid off in the body; a turn with no cause; a promise that outruns the first-week experience; offer arithmetic that differs between close, checkout and emails.

### 9.5 Tonal tells and non-tells

Tonal: assistant voice ("you may want to consider," "it's worth noting"); over-explaining that kills curiosity; cheerful neutrality with no enemy and no stance; flattery ("You deserve this"); mimicked voice with no lived story; stock metaphors; apology at the pitch transition; hype to finance and research buyers (Lavender).

Not reliable tells: perfect grammar; bland or formal prose alone; transition words; contractions; exclamation marks; ellipses; ALL-CAPS words; fragments; "Here's what / how / why"; "Dear Friend"; "P.S."; "not just X but Y"; "actually"; "Look," openers; rule of three and "Imagine" by presence; em dashes at or below human rates. Never reward "sounds casual" as a proxy for "sounds human." Score clusters.

## 10. The copy chief's rubric

### 10.1 The posture

The reviewer predicts whether a skeptical stranger sends money and keeps it. Hopkins (verified): "Treat it as a salesman. Force it to justify itself." Rogers (Copy Chief show notes): in a typical first-draft review "there's something critical on the very first page that needs to be fixed... so I rarely get beyond that before sending over a list of notes." The review stops at the first fatal defect. Every review is against a control or the strongest competitor promotion; the output is beat, lose or unsure with one reason, logged against the result. The 36 critique pairs in `research/critique_pairs_v0.json` show the chiefs' first cut is the hook: Deutsch's only note on Goff's anti-aging email was that it "sounded like every other anti-aging email in his inbox"; the rehook ("V8 Juice worse for your heart than McDonald's french fries?") did about $15,400 on a $3,500 drop (secondhand).

### 10.2 The cold read

A persona in the same model with the brief removed is a warm read with its memory wiped. The cold read runs on a different model with no brief, memo or ledger. It reads the skim path first (headline, deck, crossheads, bold lines, captions, P.S., order form, extracted and read alone) and reports what is sold, to whom, for how much, and why believe it. Then it reads the full draft once, fast, and reports the line where attention dropped, the one claim it disbelieves, and whether it can repeat the promise in one sentence. A readback that does not match the argument memo fails the draft regardless of prose.

### 10.3 Ordered gates, stop at the first failure

Gate A, argument. From the draft alone the reviewer writes "This sells [product] to [one reader] on the promise [one thing], and the reason to believe it is [mechanism/proof]." Any slot blank, plural, or a mechanism dressed as a benefit (Makepeace's "Balance Blood Sugar Levels Naturally!") fails. Supporting claims are fine (Ford: the Rule of One "does not mean you're limited to only one claim"); competing central promises are not.

Gate B, belief and lead. First the belief-violation check (Schwalm): does the headline or deck assert anything the prospect does not yet accept? If so, reorder to the strongest claim the reader accepts without argument. Then the lead checks in `04-copy-standard.md` Section 1 (headline stops the specific reader; speaker by word 150; one claim a skeptic would doubt; product named where awareness allows; lead type matches the awareness field).

Gate C, claims. Every claim maps to a ledger entry at the ledger's precision; no hedge on a supported claim; every headline promise is paid off in the body with its carrier; every description of the reader's state maps to a verbatim; objections map to the research list; the offer's numbers are identical at close, checkout and in every email; the promise does not outrun the first-week experience and refund window.

Gate D, voice. Fails the read-aloud test; assistant register; "we/our" instead of a named "I" to "you"; Deutsch's "they sound like copy is expected to sound"; a "That's why we built" turn with no cause.

Alongside the gates runs the sentence-job audit: every sentence tagged claim, proof, mechanism, picture, transition, instruction or identification; untagged sentences cut; paragraphs with no claim and no proof flagged.

### 10.4 The score, demoted

The first synthesis's 100-point score with 75/60 thresholds had no source, and additive scoring across seven dimensions produces the balanced multi-note review this document names as the AI reviewer failure. The dimensions are logged for calibration against results; they never decide the verdict and are never averaged. Readability is the FK grade reported as a flag.

### 10.5 The critique format

```
VERDICT: REJECT at Gate N | REVISE | APPROVE for factual review
PREDICTION vs CONTROL: beat | lose | unsure, with one reason
ARGUMENT READBACK: "This sells ___ to ___ on the promise ___ because ___." (matches memo? Y/N)
SKIM-PATH READBACK: what the crossheads, bold and P.S. alone sell, and for how much
WHAT WORKS (2-3 items, quoted, kept on purpose)
THE ONE THING (the single defect most likely to kill response)
   Quote: "<exact line(s)>"
   Fails: <gate or principle, one sentence>
   Do: <direction; at most one example rewritten line>
SECONDARY NOTES (max 5; each quote / fails / do)
DO NOT TOUCH (lines the revise step may not "improve")
TESTABLE DISPUTES (taste, not principle: propose the one-variable A/B)
```

Forde (verified): "start with the positive. Not excessively so, not insincerely. But clearly and immediately." Copyhackers (verified): "Avoid commenting on writing style."

### 10.6 Send-it-back reasons and reviewer instructions

By frequency across the chiefs and the critique pairs: no argument or several, or the same hook as every competitor; faux benefit at the center; vague where the customer's words exist; headline describes rather than stops; lead opens by selling or explaining, or nobody knows who is talking; claims without proof; the real objection unanswered (Rogers on Loom: "we can't see what's inside"); sounds like copy; explains everything; curiosity that does not lead to the offer (Mad Mimi: 927% more clicks, no signups); offer not sold; reassurance that plants anxiety (Unbounce/Aagaard, tested: "100% privacy – We will never spam you" cut sign-ups 18.70%); "assumes facts not in evidence."

The automated reviewer gets the sales posture and the control, runs the cold read on a different model first, runs the gates in order and stops, writes the argument readback before any note, and is capped at one fatal note and five secondary; `04-copy-standard.md` Section 6 holds the full instruction. Kennedy's passes: the Library of Congress contents confirm "Rewrite for Strategy," "Rewrite for Style" and "Rewrite for Passion! Edit for Clarity!"; "structure" and "pruning" came from a summary. Run those three as separate instructions, then Sugarman's "fewest words," then Carlton's gun-to-the-head question per word.

## 11. Compliance: the real line

### 11.1 The frame the FTC uses

Deception is judged on net impression: the Commission "will examine 'the entire mosaic, rather than each tile separately,'" and "accurate information in the text may not remedy a false headline because reasonable consumers may glance only at the headline" (verified). Substantiation must exist before the claim runs, at the level implied: "When the substantiation claim is express (e.g., 'tests prove', 'doctors recommend', and 'studies show'), the Commission expects the firm to have at least the advertised level of substantiation." A disclosure "can only qualify or limit a claim to avoid a misleading impression. It cannot cure a false claim." The Commission "generally will not pursue cases involving obviously exaggerated or puffing representations," but "Claims phrased as opinions are actionable... if... the recipient reasonably interprets them as implied statements of fact." The moment a claim is specific, measurable, comparative or tied to a mechanism it needs support; vagueness is safer and weaker, which is why the ledger matters more than the lawyer.

Two claims live in every efficacy sentence (POM Wonderful v. FTC, D.C. Cir. 2015, verified): the efficacy claim needs a reasonable basis, and the establishment claim ("clinically proven," "studies show," lab coats, journal citations) must be backed by exactly the level of proof it implies. The court recorded that "preliminary," "initial" and "promising" "do not neutralize the claims made when the specific results are otherwise described in unequivocally positive terms," and that an unambiguous statement that "the evidence in support of this claim is inconclusive" or "additional research is necessary" can qualify a claim backed by evidence short of an RCT. One well-designed RCT is the ceiling for disease claims; the two-RCT requirement was struck.

### 11.2 Claims taxonomy

`04-copy-standard.md` Section 8 carries the full table: for each of nineteen claim types (specific health benefit, structure/function, establishment, results testimonial, experience testimonial, narrative, expert endorsement, specific earnings, opportunity characterization, track record, price and discount, "free," guarantee, continuity and trial, scarcity, comparative, vague superlative, ad identity, audience language) the substantiation required, the disclosure required and the prohibited forms, each with its rule cited (16 CFR 233, 255, 437, 465; 21 CFR 101.93; 17 CFR 4.41; ROSCA; the FTC 2022 dark-patterns list; Meta's personal-attributes rule). Every ledger row carries one of these types, and the factual reviewer's strength test runs against the type's required level.

### 11.3 Permitted phrasing by evidence grade

The gap-fill built a table (from the Health Products Guidance, POM, 255.2, 437.4, 4.41, the SEC Marketing Rule, FINRA 2210 and FDA qualified-claim letters) that tells the reviewer what it may write at each grade; `04-copy-standard.md` Section 8 carries it in full. The reviewer's action is always "rewrite to the highest permitted form at this grade," never "append a disclaimer." Grade A (RCT on the finished product at the marketed dose) allows establishment language with a stated n, duration and average effect against placebo, within the endpoint, population and duration measured (Example 35: a working-memory study cannot support "improves memory"). Grade B (RCT on the ingredient at the same dose and form) states the dose match and "we did not run our own trial"; a different extract (Example 38), a combination that could change the outcome (Example 37) or a lower dose fails. Grade C (observational) uses the association qualifier, in FDA's accepted form (verified): "Supportive but not conclusive research shows that eating 1.5 ounces per day of macadamia nuts... may reduce the risk of coronary heart disease." Grade D (mechanistic, in vitro, animal) may say "In lab studies, [compound] blocked the enzyme that breaks down collagen. Whether that happens in people taking [Product] has not been tested," and may use the POM-approved sentence verbatim: "The evidence in support of this claim is inconclusive; additional research is necessary." Grade E (anecdote, testimonial, traditional use) uses Example 44 verbatim, "Ancient remedy used for centuries to aid digestion. There is no scientific evidence that it works," with the second sentence "as prominent and legible as the first," and pairs any result testimonial with the typical result; "Results not typical" alone, a range in place of a central figure, and completer-only averages fail. Grade F (no evidence) sells on ingredients and amounts, sourcing, manufacturing standard, taste, guarantee, price, delivery and who it is for, and may still sell hard on reason-why, guarantee and scarcity with a basis.

Hedge words the regulators treat as decoration: "may," "might," "could," "helps," "supports," "promotes," "promising," "preliminary," "initial," "Results not typical," "Individual results may vary," "studies suggest" without a study. Hedges that count: "the evidence is inconclusive," "additional research is necessary," "there is no scientific evidence that it works," "supportive but not conclusive research shows," a stated n, duration, dose, population and average effect, and "not tested on this product; the study used the isolated ingredient at X mg." A counted hedge sits in the same sentence or immediately adjacent at the same prominence, with no testimonial or image in the same section re-asserting the full claim.

### 11.4 Testimonials, earnings and financial copy

16 CFR 255.2 (verified): a consumer endorsement "will be interpreted as representing that the product is effective for the purpose depicted," and "Consumer endorsements themselves are not competent and reliable scientific evidence." Example 2: "Results not typical" and "you are not likely to have similar results" are insufficient. Example 4: when users typically lose 15 pounds in six months, "most women... lose between 10 and 50 pounds" is inadequate, as is a completer-only figure "because it does not communicate what the typical outcome is for users who start the program." The accepted form (Health Guidance Example 41): "In an 8-week study, subjects taking the supplement lost an average of 5 pounds. Subjects taking a placebo lost an average of 1 pound," placed "immediately adjacent to the quote and in prominent font." "Paid link" adjacent is an adequate connection disclosure; a disclosure in comments or behind a "LEGAL" link is not.

Earnings: 16 CFR 437.1(f) (verified) covers "Any statements from which a prospective purchaser can reasonably infer that he or she will earn a minimum level of income (e.g., 'earn enough to buy a Porsche,' 'earn a six-figure income,' or 'earn your investment back within one year')," and the April 2026 LifeWave order bars misrepresentation "including through images of homes, vehicles, purchases, or travel." The 2021 MMO Notice lists earnings claims made "absent clear and conspicuous disclosure of the relevant context, such as the time and effort actually expended... the percentage of participants making the amount represented, and the amount typically and ordinarily made," plus false scarcity, "no experience" and "risk-free" claims; civil penalties attach only to firms that received the Notice. Permitted form: "Jen made $4,300 in her third month. Of the 1,912 people who joined between Jan 2024 and Jun 2025, 6% reached $4,000 in any month; the median member made $210 in their first 90 days, working about 5 hours a week. Ad spend is not included." The verified cases are WealthPress ("potentially make $24,840 dollars—or more—every single week," trades that "were often not real," disclaimers "so far removed from the claims"; $1.2M refunds and a $500K penalty) and Agora Financial ("permanently cure type 2 diabetes in 28 days"; "Republican Checks" for a book that "merely describes an investment strategy focused on stocks that pay dividends"). In every action the FTC objected to numbers that were not real, not typical or not contextualized and to disclaimers detached from the claim, never to specificity, strong promises or urgency as such. The fix is adjacency.

Financial copy adds three regimes: the CFTC 4.41(b) statement verbatim for hypothetical results, "prominently disclosed and in immediate proximity"; net with gross "with equal prominence" and "fair and balanced treatment of any associated material risks" under the SEC Marketing Rule; and FINRA 2210, which bars statements that "predict or project performance, imply that past performance will recur." The newsletter publisher's exclusion (Lowe v. SEC) remains open.

### 11.5 Over-cautious patterns, channel rules and the factual-review step

Stop: "may help," "can potentially," "could possibly" on supported claims; "Results may vary" anywhere (the 2009 Guides removed the safe harbor; use the typical result once, adjacent); disclaimers mid-argument; deleting the specific number because "numbers are risky"; refusing comparative claims; softening a real guarantee; the DSHEA disclaimer in emails and ads (a labeling requirement that cures nothing in an ad); "This is not financial advice" after every sentence (one clear statement at the first recommendation, risks adjacent to the claim, as the Agora order requires); refusing true urgency; hedging a mechanism the ledger supports; stripping experience testimonials ("I stopped hiding from photos"); inventing objections "to address compliance."

Channels: CAN-SPAM (verified) requires accurate subject lines, ad identification, a postal address and an opt-out honored within ten business days, with penalties "up to $53,088" per email; Meta, Google, native and SMS rules are in Section 5; California B&P 17602 governs subscriptions sold into California. Non-US regimes, TikTok and LinkedIn ad policies and Amazon's bullet rules were not researched.

Factual review: each flag is `line → rule (cited) → rewritten line`, and a flag with no rewrite is rejected. Tests in order: ledger match (every number, study, credential, date, quote, name, price, former price, deadline and quantity; missing → the ledger-supported version or a visible `[[NEEDS SOURCE]]`); strength match (implied proof level against ledger grade, rewritten to the permitted form); disease screen for supplements; testimonial pack (real person on file, bona fide user, typical result adjacent, connection disclosed); earnings screen; track-record screen; net impression pass on headline, lead and offer only, fixing the headline and never the footer; offer mechanics and the cross-asset consistency check; channel pass; fabrication guard (any name, quote, statistic, study, credential, review or narrator not in the ledger is a hard fail). The reviewer may not add "may," add "results vary," add generic disclaimers, delete specifics the ledger supports, or raise "could be seen as" concerns without a rule; it may add an evidence-state sentence.

## 13. Implications for the engine

### 13.1 Pipeline order

The first synthesis's pipeline (research → argument → draft → editorial → factual → revise) repeats the AI failure it diagnoses: it inherits the offer and judges prose before the claims are matched. Revised: research (dossier, competitor table, proof hunt, offer audit) → offer construction (options sheet, chosen variant, every term a ledger row) → argument selection (anchored rubric, three candidates, two losers kept) → order form and close drafted first from the offer block → bullets and proof blocks → body → three leads, cut from the top, cold read on a different model, winner rewritten → headline and deck → claims matched inline (every claim carries a ledger ID) → editorial review (cold read, gates, control prediction) → factual and compliance review (the join on ledger IDs, both directions) → revise under DO NOT TOUCH → ship as control plus one challenger lead with the single variable named → log the prediction and the result, including refunds at 30 days.

### 13.2 Step outputs

Research: the tagged verbatim table; ranked themes with three verbatims each; the competitor table with the logo-swap result; awareness and sophistication per segment; the proof inventory graded A-F; the objection map from VOC only; the resident emotion; the story inventory; the speaker object; the first-week experience and refund history. Offer construction: the options sheet, the chosen variant with reasons, guarantee denominated, deadline with mechanism, the promise/delivery gap note. Argument selection: three candidates with lead verbatim, belief to displace, mechanism mapped to ledger rows and proof asset; gate results; the winning thesis ("This draft exists to prove that [mechanism] is a different and superior way to get [promise] for [reader], and the proof is [carrier]"); the two losers as body reasons and next lead tests. Draft: the offer block rendered as order form and close first; three leads with cold-read readbacks; the skim path extracted; inline ledger IDs. Editorial: the critique schema with the control prediction. Factual: flags with rewrites; the consistency check. Ship: control, challenger lead, one-variable test plan, prediction logged.

### 13.3 Draft prompts

Per-format, per-section prompts from the format spec, never one "write the sales page" prompt. Each carries role, quality criteria, hard bans and required format, with the dossier, thesis, proof map with ledger IDs, offer block, voice profile and three exemplar paragraphs in context; never tone adjectives. Drafting blocks until the model has listed missing mechanism, missing proof and unclear awareness and each is answered or marked unknown. Rules to encode are the numbered standard in `04-copy-standard.md` Section 1 (speaker by word 150, one claim a skeptic would doubt in the lead, every claim's carrier within two sentences, objections only from the map, one admission from the brief, one CTA path, the turn as a reason-why sentence). Forbid the Word-Substitute Technique (Schwartz, verified: "the shallowest, and the most widespread and ineffective"). Headlines and leads are found by search first (the unused fact, the opposite idea), with three to five variants per unit and selection as the tiebreaker.

### 13.4 Reviewers, ledger and specs

Editorial: Section 10 as written, with the cold read on a different model and the control prediction logged. Mechanical passes: the tiered detector with the ledger match as Tier 0 and vocabulary as Tier 3; the per-format cadence floor; FK grade; the "and then" beat test; format hard gates (SMS characters, cold-email words, bump words, first-125-characters standalone, first-5-seconds spoken). Factual: the Section 11.5 procedure with the 11.3 phrasing table, so the reviewer states the smaller true claim instead of stripping it.

The ledger row fields and the per-offer config are listed in `04-copy-standard.md` Section 8. Format specs as machine-readable section jobs, bands, must-contain lists and failure flags, with length computed from awareness, sophistication, price and segment count; a funnel object carrying one argument across ad, opt-in, email, page, order form and upsell, with a reviewer check that reads only the first screen of each asset (spec open).

### 13.5 Calibration

Count every tell in the assembled human control corpus and the library examples against engine drafts before setting thresholds; the current numbers are Claude-only on the AI side and four authors on the human side. Log which candidate argument won and its downstream conversion and refund rate. Keep the reviewer's prediction scorecard. Run the first three A/B tests (engine against control for a lead, an email and an ad) with sample sizes computed, one variable per test. Build the control spec (format, traffic, diagnosed awareness and sophistication, argument sentence, lead type, mechanism, proof placement, offer devices, result metrics with dates and sample sizes, losing variants, rights status) and apply it to every acquired control, so the library is calibrated across markets and formats rather than to one publisher's house style. Calibrate the judge on documented split-test pairs: it must pick the recorded winner on held-out pairs before it adjudicates anything.
